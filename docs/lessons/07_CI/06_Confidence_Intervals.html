Lesson 6: Confidence Intervals

Review: Where We Left Off

Key concepts from Lessons 1-5:

  • Point estimation: \(\hat{\theta}\) estimates unknown parameter \(\theta\)
  • Bias and MSE: Properties for evaluating estimators
  • MVUE: Minimum variance unbiased estimators
  • MLE: Maximum likelihood estimation
  • Sufficient statistics: Data reduction without information loss

. . .

Today’s Goals:

  • Move from point estimates to interval estimates
  • Understand the concept of confidence level
  • Derive confidence intervals using pivotal quantities
  • Explore the relationship between precision and confidence

From Point Estimates to Intervals

A point estimate like \(\bar{x} = 11.28\) tells us nothing about precision or reliability.

. . .

Consider: A clinical trial testing a new blood pressure medication

# Blood pressure reductions (mmHg) from 25 patients
bp_reductions <- c(12.3, 8.7, 15.2, 10.1, 7.4, 14.8, 11.2, 9.5, 13.1, 16.2,
                   8.1, 12.5, 10.8, 14.3, 11.7, 9.2, 15.0, 13.4, 7.9, 12.1,
                   10.5, 8.3, 14.1, 11.5, 13.8)

cat("Point estimate of mean BP reduction:", round(mean(bp_reductions), 2), "mmHg\n")
Point estimate of mean BP reduction: 11.67 mmHg

. . .

The Key Question

How close is \(\bar{x} = 11.28\) mmHg to the true population mean \(\mu\)? Could we get a different answer with different patients?

The Confidence Interval Approach

Instead of a single number, we report an interval of plausible values.

. . .

Definition: A confidence interval (CI) is a range of values that, with a specified level of confidence, contains the true parameter value.

. . .

Two Key Properties of CIs
  1. Confidence level: How reliable is the interval? (e.g., 95%)
  2. Width: How precise is our estimate? (narrower = more precise)

. . .

The tradeoff: Higher confidence → Wider interval → Less precision

Deriving the CI for \(\mu\) (Known \(\sigma\))

Setup: The Idealized Scenario

Assumptions:

  1. \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) is a random sample from a normal population
  2. The population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is known

. . .

While \(\sigma\) known is unrealistic, this case:

  • Illustrates the fundamental concepts
  • Provides the foundation for more realistic scenarios
  • Is approximately valid when \(n\) is very large

Starting Point: The Sampling Distribution

From the Central Limit Theorem and properties of normal distributions:

\[\bar{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\]

. . .

Standardizing:

\[Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0, 1)\]

. . .

This \(Z\) has a known distribution (standard normal) that does not depend on \(\mu\).

The Pivotal Quantity

Definition (Devore 8.1)

A pivotal quantity is a random variable \(h(X_1, \ldots, X_n; \theta)\) such that:

  1. It is a function of both the data and the unknown parameter \(\theta\)
  2. Its probability distribution does not depend on \(\theta\) or any other unknown parameters

. . .

Our pivotal quantity:

\[Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\]

  • ✓ Depends on data (\(\bar{X}\)) and unknown parameter (\(\mu\))
  • ✓ Distribution is \(N(0, 1)\) — doesn’t depend on \(\mu\)

Building the Interval: Step 1

For the standard normal distribution, we know:

\[P(-1.96 < Z < 1.96) = 0.95\]

. . .

Code 
tibble(z = seq(-4, 4, 0.01)) |>

  theme(legend.position = "top")

Building the Interval: Step 2

Substitute our pivotal quantity:

\[P\left(-1.96 < \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} < 1.96\right) = 0.95\]

. . .

Isolate \(\mu\): Multiply all parts by \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\):

\[P\left(-1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \bar{X} - \mu < 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 0.95\]

. . .

Rearrange (subtract \(\bar{X}\), multiply by \(-1\)):

\[P\left(\bar{X} - 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu < \bar{X} + 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 0.95\]

The Random Interval

Key Insight

The interval \(\left(\bar{X} - 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{X} + 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\) is random because \(\bar{X}\) is random.

. . .

Before sampling: With probability 0.95, this random interval will contain \(\mu\).

After sampling: We observe \(\bar{x}\) and compute the interval. The interval is now fixed — it either contains \(\mu\) or it doesn’t.

. . .

The 95% Confidence Interval

Definition (Devore 8.1)

A 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\) (with known \(\sigma\)) is:

\[\left(\bar{x} - 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{x} + 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\]

or equivalently: \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

. . .

Components:

  • \(\bar{x}\) = point estimate (center of the interval)
  • \(1.96 \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}\) = margin of error
  • \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) = standard error of \(\bar{X}\)

Example: Titanium Alloy Tensile Strength (Devore 8.1-8.2)

Problem: An engineer wants to estimate the true mean tensile strength of a titanium alloy weld. Previous tests suggest \(\sigma = 55\) MPa. A sample of \(n = 31\) welds gives \(\bar{x} = 1064\) MPa.

. . .

Calculate the 95% CI:

sigma <- 55
n <- 31
xbar <- 1064
z_crit <- 1.96

margin_error <- z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n)
lower <- xbar - margin_error
upper <- xbar + margin_error

cat("95% CI: (", round(lower, 1), ",", round(upper, 1), ") MPa\n")
95% CI: ( 1044.6 , 1083.4 ) MPa
cat("Margin of Error:", round(margin_error, 1), "MPa\n")
Margin of Error: 19.4 MPa

. . .

Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true mean tensile strength is between 1044.6 and 1083.4 MPa.

Medical Example: Blood Pressure Medication

Scenario: A clinical trial measures systolic BP reduction (mmHg) for a new medication. Assume \(\sigma = 5\) mmHg (from prior studies). With \(n = 25\) patients:

# Data
bp_reductions <- c(12.3, 8.7, 15.2, 10.1, 7.4, 14.8, 11.2, 9.5, 13.1, 16.2,
                   8.1, 12.5, 10.8, 14.3, 11.7, 9.2, 15.0, 13.4, 7.9, 12.1,
                   10.5, 8.3, 14.1, 11.5, 13.8)
sigma <- 5
n <- length(bp_reductions)
xbar <- mean(bp_reductions)

# 95% CI
margin_error <- 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n)
ci_lower <- xbar - margin_error
ci_upper <- xbar + margin_error

cat("Sample mean:", round(xbar, 2), "mmHg\n")
Sample mean: 11.67 mmHg
cat("95% CI: (", round(ci_lower, 2), ",", round(ci_upper, 2), ") mmHg\n")
95% CI: ( 9.71 , 13.63 ) mmHg

. . .

Clinical interpretation: We’re 95% confident the medication reduces systolic BP by between 9.32 and 13.24 mmHg on average.

Interpreting Confidence Level

What Does “95% Confident” Mean?

Common Misconception

WRONG: “There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) lies in the interval (9.32, 13.24).”

. . .

Why is this wrong?

  • \(\mu\) is a fixed (though unknown) constant
  • The interval (9.32, 13.24) is fixed after we compute it
  • There’s no probability involved — \(\mu\) either is or isn’t in this specific interval

The Correct Interpretation

Frequentist Interpretation

If we were to repeat this sampling procedure many times:

  • Each time computing a 95% CI from a new random sample
  • 95% of those intervals would contain the true \(\mu\)

. . .

In other words: The method works 95% of the time, not this specific interval.

. . .

Think of it as confidence in the procedure, not in the specific interval.

Simulation: Understanding Confidence Level

Code 
set.seed(2024)
mu_true <- 11  # True mean BP reduction
sigma <- 5
n <- 25
n_samples <- 100

# Generate 100 confidence intervals
ci_sim <- tibble(sample_id = 1:n_samples) |>
  mutate(
    data = map(sample_id, ~rnorm(n, mu_true, sigma)),
    xbar = map_dbl(data, mean),
    lower = xbar - 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
    upper = xbar + 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
    contains_mu = lower < mu_true & upper > mu_true
  )

coverage <- mean(ci_sim$contains_mu)

# Plot
ci_sim |>
  slice_head(n = 50) |>
  ggplot(aes(y = sample_id)) +
  geom_segment(aes(x = lower, xend = upper, yend = sample_id, color = contains_mu),
               linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_point(aes(x = xbar, color = contains_mu), size = 1.5) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mu_true, color = "red", linewidth = 1.2, linetype = "dashed") +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("FALSE" = "orange", "TRUE" = "steelblue"),
                     labels = c("Misses μ", "Contains μ")) +
  labs(title = paste0("50 of ", n_samples, " Simulated 95% CIs"),
       subtitle = paste0("Coverage: ", round(100*coverage, 1), "% contain true μ = ", mu_true),
       x = "BP Reduction (mmHg)", y = "Sample Number", color = "") +
  theme(legend.position = "top")

cat("Expected coverage: 95%\n")

Practical Interpretation

When we report a 95% CI, we’re using a reliable method. About 1 in 20 times, we’ll be unlucky and our interval won’t contain the true value — but we won’t know which times those are!

Other Confidence Levels

General Confidence Interval Formula

To construct a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI, we use \(z_{\alpha/2}\) — the critical value that captures upper tail area \(\alpha/2\).

. . .

General CI for \(\mu\) (Known \(\sigma\))

\[\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

where \(P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \alpha/2\) for \(Z \sim N(0,1)\).

. . .

Confidence Level \(\alpha\) \(\alpha/2\) \(z_{\alpha/2}\)
90% 0.10 0.05 1.645
95% 0.05 0.025 1.960
99% 0.01 0.005 2.576

Example: Comparing Confidence Levels (Devore 8.3)

sigma <- 13

Confidence Level, Precision, and Sample Size

The Width of a Confidence Interval

The width of a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI is:

\[w = 2 \cdot z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

. . .

Width depends on:

Factor Effect on Width
\(\uparrow\) Confidence level Width increases (larger \(z_{\alpha/2}\))
\(\uparrow\) Sample size \(n\) Width decreases (larger \(\sqrt{n}\))
\(\uparrow\) Population SD \(\sigma\) Width increases

Sample Size Determination

The question: How large a sample do we need to achieve a desired width \(w\) at a specified confidence level?

. . .

From \(w = 2 \cdot z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\), solving for \(n\):

Sample Size Formula (Devore 8.6)

\[n = \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma}{w/2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2 \cdot z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma}{w}\right)^2\]

where \(w/2\) is the desired margin of error.

Example: Response Time Study (Devore 8.4)

Problem: A new operating system is installed. Response time is normally distributed with \(\sigma = 25\) ms. What sample size ensures a 95% CI with width at most 10 ms?

. . .

sigma <- 25
w <- 10  # desired width
z_crit <- 1.96

n_required <- (2 * z_crit * sigma / w)^2
cat("Required sample size:", ceiling(n_required), "\n")
Required sample size: 97

. . .

Check:

n <- 97
actual_width <- 2 * z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n)
cat("Actual width with n =", n, ":", round(actual_width, 2), "ms\n")
Actual width with n = 97 : 9.95 ms

Medical Example: Planning a Clinical Trial

Scenario: Planning a trial for a hypertension drug. Prior studies suggest \(\sigma = 8\) mmHg for BP reduction. You want to estimate the true mean reduction to within 2 mmHg with 99% confidence.

sigma <- 8
margin_error <- 2  # desired margin of error
z_crit <- 2.576  # for 99% CI

n_required <- (z_crit * sigma / margin_error)^2
cat("For 99% CI with margin of error ±2 mmHg:\n")
For 99% CI with margin of error ±2 mmHg:
cat("Required sample size:", ceiling(n_required), "patients\n")
Required sample size: 107 patients

. . .

What if we use 95% confidence instead?

z_crit_95 <- 1.96
n_required_95 <- (z_crit_95 * sigma / margin_error)^2
cat("\nFor 95% CI with same margin of error:\n")

For 95% CI with same margin of error:
cat("Required sample size:", ceiling(n_required_95), "patients\n")
Required sample size: 62 patients
cat("Savings:", ceiling(n_required) - ceiling(n_required_95), "patients\n")
Savings: 45 patients

Sample Size vs. Precision

Code 
# Show how sample size affects width
sample_sizes <- seq(10, 200, 5)
sigma <- 25

width_data <- tibble(n = sample_sizes) |>
  mutate(
    width_90 = 2 * 1.645 * sigma / sqrt(n),
    width_95 = 2 * 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
    width_99 = 2 * 2.576 * sigma / sqrt(n)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(starts_with("width"), names_to = "conf_level", values_to = "width") |>
  mutate(conf_level = case_when(
    conf_level == "width_90" ~ "90%",
    conf_level == "width_95" ~ "95%",
    conf_level == "width_99" ~ "99%"
  ))

ggplot(width_data, aes(x = n, y = width, color = conf_level)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 10, linetype = "dashed", color = "gray50") +
  annotate("text", x = 180, y = 11, label = "Target width = 10", size = 4) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("90%" = "darkblue", "95%" = "steelblue", "99%" = "lightblue")) +
  labs(title = "CI Width vs. Sample Size",
       subtitle = "σ = 25, showing diminishing returns",
       x = "Sample Size (n)", y = "CI Width", color = "Confidence\nLevel") +
  theme(legend.position = "right")

One-Sided Confidence Intervals

Confidence Bounds

Sometimes we only need a lower bound or upper bound for the parameter.

. . .

Examples:

  • A surgeon wants only a lower bound for average remission time after cancer surgery
  • A manufacturer wants only an upper bound for average defect rate
  • An engineer wants only a lower bound for mean tensile strength

Derivation of One-Sided Bounds

For a 95% lower confidence bound:

\[P(Z < 1.645) = 0.95\]

\[P\left(\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} < 1.645\right) = 0.95\]

. . .

Rearranging:

\[P\left(\bar{X} - 1.645 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu\right) = 0.95\]

One-Sided Confidence Bounds
  • 95% Lower Bound: \(\bar{x} - 1.645 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu\)
  • 95% Upper Bound: \(\mu < \bar{x} + 1.645 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Example: Tensile Strength Lower Bound (Devore 8.7)

Problem: For the titanium alloy weld data (\(\bar{x} = 1064\) MPa, \(\sigma = 55\) MPa, \(n = 31\)), compute a 95% lower confidence bound.

xbar <- 1064
sigma <- 55
n <- 31
z_crit <- 1.645  # for one-sided 95%

lower_bound <- xbar - z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n)
cat("95% Lower Confidence Bound:", round(lower_bound, 2), "MPa\n")
95% Lower Confidence Bound: 1047.75 MPa

. . .

Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true mean tensile strength is at least 1047.75 MPa.

. . .

Compare with two-sided 95% CI: (1044.6, 1083.4) — the lower bound is higher than the two-sided lower limit!

Simulation: Properties of CIs

Understanding CI Coverage Through Simulation

Let’s simulate many samples and examine how often CIs capture the true parameter.

Code 
set.seed(551)
mu_true <- 100
sigma <- 15
n_values <- c(10, 25, 50, 100)
n_sims <- 1000

# Function to compute CI and check coverage
simulate_coverage <- function(n, mu, sigma, n_sims) {
  tibble(sim = 1:n_sims) |>
    mutate(
      xbar = map_dbl(sim, ~mean(rnorm(n, mu, sigma))),
      lower = xbar - 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
      upper = xbar + 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
      contains_mu = lower < mu & upper > mu
    )
}

coverage_results <- tibble(n = n_values) |>
  mutate(
    results = map(n, ~simulate_coverage(.x, mu_true, sigma, n_sims)),
    coverage = map_dbl(results, ~mean(.x$contains_mu)),
    avg_width = map_dbl(results, ~mean(.x$upper - .x$lower))
  )

coverage_results |>
  select(n, coverage, avg_width) |>
  mutate(coverage = paste0(round(100*coverage, 1), "%"),
         avg_width = round(avg_width, 2)) |>
  knitr::kable(col.names = c("Sample Size", "Coverage", "Average Width"))
Sample Size Coverage Average Width
10 93.8% 18.59
25 95% 11.76
50 95.3% 8.32
100 95.3% 5.88

Code 
coverage_results |>
  unnest(results) |>
  filter(sim <= 50) |>
  ggplot(aes(y = sim)) +
  geom_segment(aes(x = lower, xend = upper, yend = sim, color = contains_mu),
               linewidth = 0.6) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mu_true, color = "red", linewidth = 1, linetype = "dashed") +
  facet_wrap(~paste("n =", n), nrow = 1, scales = "free_x") +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("FALSE" = "orange", "TRUE" = "steelblue"),
                     labels = c("Misses μ", "Contains μ")) +
  labs(title = "95% CIs for Different Sample Sizes (first 50 simulations)",
       subtitle = paste0("True μ = ", mu_true, " (red dashed line)"),
       x = "Value", y = "Simulation", color = "") +
  theme(legend.position = "top")

Key Observations

What the Simulation Shows
  1. Coverage is approximately 95% regardless of sample size (when assumptions hold)
  2. Width decreases with sample size — larger samples give more precise estimates
  3. Width decreases proportional to \(1/\sqrt{n}\) — need to quadruple \(n\) to halve the width
  4. Intervals are centered at \(\bar{x}\), which varies from sample to sample

Summary

Lesson 6: Key Takeaways

1. Confidence Intervals provide interval estimates

  • More informative than point estimates alone
  • Communicate both estimate and precision

. . .

2. The pivotal quantity method

\[Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)\]

  • Allows derivation of CI formula
  • Foundation for more complex scenarios

. . .

3. The \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\mu\) (known \(\sigma\)):

\[\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

Key Formulas

Quantity Formula
95% CI \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
General CI \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
CI Width \(w = 2 \cdot z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Sample Size \(n = \left(\frac{2z_{\alpha/2}\sigma}{w}\right)^2\)
Lower Bound \(\bar{x} - z_\alpha \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Upper Bound \(\bar{x} + z_\alpha \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
