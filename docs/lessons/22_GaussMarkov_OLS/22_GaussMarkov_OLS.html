The Simple Linear Regression Model

Model (DBC §12.1)

\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, n\]

where:

  • \(Y_i\) = response (random variable) for the \(i\)-th observation
  • \(x_i\) = predictor value (fixed, non-random), in a fixed-design setup
  • \(\beta_0\) = true intercept (unknown parameter)
  • \(\beta_1\) = true slope (unknown parameter)
  • \(\varepsilon_i\) = random error term

Notice: The \(x_i\)’s are not random; we treat them as known constants.

This is different from our earlier iid setup! The \(Y_i\)’s are independent but not identically distributed: each has a different mean \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\).

The Gauss-Markov Theorem: Our Destination

Before examining the assumptions, let’s state the theorem we are building toward.

Gauss-Markov Theorem (preview)

Under four conditions on the error terms \(\varepsilon_i\), the OLS estimators \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\).

That is, among all estimators that are (1) linear functions of \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) and (2) unbiased, OLS has the smallest variance.

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1^{\text{OLS}}) \leq \text{Var}(\tilde\beta_1) \quad \text{for every linear unbiased } \tilde\beta_1\]

Why does this matter? This is the regression analog of everything we built in Lessons 1–6: MVUE, CRLB, sufficiency. The four conditions (the Gauss-Markov assumptions) are exactly what makes OLS optimal. Understanding them tells you when OLS is trustworthy, and what to do when it is not.

The Gauss-Markov Assumptions

Gauss-Markov (GM) Assumptions

  1. Linearity: \(E[Y_i] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\) (the mean follows a line)
  2. Independence: \(\varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_n\) are independent
  3. Zero mean: \(E[\varepsilon_i] = 0\) for all \(i\)
  4. Homoscedasticity: \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\) (same variance; no normality required!)

Key point: The Gauss-Markov theorem does not require normality.

Under these four assumptions alone, OLS is BLUE.

Under the additional assumption \(\varepsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2)\), OLS is even stronger: it equals the MLE and is the MVUE, not just BLUE.

Part 2: The OLS Estimators: Derivation

The Principle of Least Squares

Question: Given data \((x_1, Y_1), \ldots, (x_n, Y_n)\), how do we choose \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\)?

Principle of Least Squares (DBC §12.1)

Choose \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) to minimize the residual sum of squares:

\[\text{RSS}(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\right)^2\]

This minimizes the total squared vertical distance from each data point to the line.

Why squares? Calculus works cleanly; squared deviations penalize large errors more than small ones; connects to maximum likelihood under normality (see later).

Deriving the OLS Estimates: Normal Equations

Take partial derivatives and set to zero:

\[\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_0} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) = 0\]

\[\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_1} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) = 0\]

These simplify to the normal equations (DBC §12.1):

\[n\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \sum x_i = \sum Y_i\]

\[\hat\beta_0 \sum x_i + \hat\beta_1 \sum x_i^2 = \sum x_i Y_i\]

Solving the Normal Equations

From the first equation: \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar{Y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x}\)

Substituting into the second and simplifying:

\[\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\]

OLS Estimators (DBC §12.1)

\[\hat\beta_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\]

\[\hat\beta_0 = \bar{Y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x}\]

where \(S_{xx} = \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\) and \(S_{xy} = \sum(x_i - \bar x)(Y_i - \bar Y)\).

Computing OLS in Our Example

# Manual computation of OLS estimators

Part 3: Properties of OLS Estimators

Step 1: OLS Estimators Are Linear in \(Y\)

Key rewrite: The slope estimator can be written as a linear combination of the \(Y_i\)’s:

\[\hat\beta_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(Y_i - \bar Y)}{S_{xx}} = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i Y_i\]

where \(c_i = \dfrac{x_i - \bar x}{S_{xx}}\) are fixed constants (they depend only on the \(x_i\)’s, not on the \(Y_i\)’s).

Similarly, \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar{Y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x} = \sum_{i=1}^n d_i Y_i\) where \(d_i = \frac{1}{n} - c_i \bar x\).

Definition: Linear Estimator

An estimator \(\tilde\beta\) is linear if it can be written as \(\tilde\beta = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i Y_i\) for some constants \(a_i\) that depend only on the \(x_i\)’s.

OLS estimators satisfy this definition; they are linear estimators.

Step 2: OLS Estimators Are Unbiased

Both \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) are unbiased estimators of \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), respectively, under the GM assumptions.

For \(\hat\beta_1\): Since \(\hat\beta_1 = \sum c_i Y_i\) and \(E[Y_i] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\):

\[E[\hat\beta_1] = E\left[\sum c_i Y_i\right] = \sum c_i E[Y_i] = \sum c_i (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i)\]

\[= \beta_0 \underbrace{\sum c_i}_{=0} + \beta_1 \underbrace{\sum c_i x_i}_{=1} = \beta_1 \checkmark\]

The two facts that make this work, for \(c_i = (x_i - \bar x)/S_{xx}\):

\[\sum_{i=1}^n c_i = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)}{S_{xx}} = \frac{0}{S_{xx}} = 0\]

because deviations from the mean always sum to zero.

\[\sum_{i=1}^n c_i x_i = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)x_i}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(x_i - \bar x) + \bar x \sum(x_i - \bar x)}{S_{xx}} = \frac{S_{xx} + 0}{S_{xx}} = 1\]

because \(\sum(x_i - \bar x)x_i = \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2 = S_{xx}\).

For \(\hat\beta_0\): \(E[\hat\beta_0] = E[\bar Y] - \hat\beta_1 \bar x = (\beta_0 + \beta_1 \bar x) - \beta_1 \bar x = \beta_0 \checkmark\)

Step 3: Exact Variances of OLS Estimators

Under the GM assumptions (\(\varepsilon_i\) independent with \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\)):

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \text{Var}\left(\sum c_i Y_i\right) = \sum c_i^2 \text{Var}(Y_i) = \sigma^2 \sum c_i^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\]

since \(\sum c_i^2 = \sum \dfrac{(x_i - \bar x)^2}{S_{xx}^2} = \dfrac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}^2} = \dfrac{1}{S_{xx}}\).

Variance Formulas (DBC §12.2)

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\]

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) = \sigma^2 \left(\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\right)\]

\[\text{Cov}(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1) = -\frac{\sigma^2 \bar x}{S_{xx}}\]

Intuition: What Drives Variance of \(\hat\beta_1\)?

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2}\]

Part 4: The Gauss-Markov Theorem

Statement of the Gauss-Markov Theorem

Gauss-Markov Theorem (DBC §12.2)

Under the four Gauss-Markov assumptions (linearity, independence, zero-mean errors, homoscedasticity), the OLS estimators \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\).

That is, among all estimators of the form \(\tilde\beta_1 = \sum a_i Y_i\) that satisfy \(E[\tilde\beta_1] = \beta_1\):

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) \leq \text{Var}(\tilde\beta_1)\]

with equality iff \(\tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1\).

Unpacking “BLUE”:

  • Best = smallest variance among the class considered
  • Linear = estimator is a linear function of the \(Y_i\)’s
  • Unbiased = \(E[\tilde\beta] = \beta\) for all \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma^2\)
  • Estimator = a function of the observed data

Proof: Gauss-Markov for \(\hat\beta_1\)

Setup: Let \(\tilde\beta_1 = \sum a_i Y_i\) be any linear unbiased estimator of \(\beta_1\), where the \(a_i\)’s are constants.

Unbiasedness constraint:

\[E[\tilde\beta_1] = \sum a_i E[Y_i] = \sum a_i(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i) = \beta_0 \sum a_i + \beta_1 \sum a_i x_i = \beta_1\]

This must hold for all \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), so: \(\quad \sum a_i = 0 \quad\) and \(\quad \sum a_i x_i = 1\).

Write \(a_i = c_i + e_i\) where \(c_i = (x_i - \bar x)/S_{xx}\) are the OLS weights and \(e_i = a_i - c_i\).

The constraints \(\sum a_i = 0\) and \(\sum a_i x_i = 1\) imply \(\sum e_i = 0\) and \(\sum e_i x_i = 0\).

Variance of \(\tilde\beta_1\):

\[\text{Var}(\tilde\beta_1) = \sigma^2 \sum a_i^2 = \sigma^2 \sum (c_i + e_i)^2 = \sigma^2\left(\sum c_i^2 + 2\sum c_i e_i + \sum e_i^2\right)\]

Since \(\sum c_i e_i = \frac{1}{S_{xx}}\sum(x_i - \bar x)e_i = \frac{1}{S_{xx}}\left(\sum x_i e_i - \bar x \sum e_i\right) = 0\), we get:

\[\text{Var}(\tilde\beta_1) = \underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}}_{\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1)} + \underbrace{\sigma^2 \sum e_i^2}_{\geq 0} \geq \text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) \quad \checkmark\]

The BLUE Hierarchy: Connecting to MVUE

  • Gauss-Markov (no normality needed): OLS is BLUE — smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators
  • Add normality: two independent arguments both conclude OLS is MVUE — see next slide

From BLUE to MVUE: Two Routes Under Normality

Adding \(\varepsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0,\sigma^2)\) unlocks two separate arguments, each concluding OLS is the MVUE.

Route 1: Lehmann-Scheffé

Under the normal SLR model, \((\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1, S^2)\) is a complete sufficient statistic for \((\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma^2)\).

\(\hat\beta_1\) is an unbiased function of that complete sufficient statistic.

Therefore, by the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem, \(\hat\beta_1\) is the unique MVUE of \(\beta_1\).

The CRLB plays no role in this argument — it follows from completeness and sufficiency alone.

Route 2: CRLB / Efficiency

The normal SLR model belongs to the exponential family. For exponential family models, the MLE achieves the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound exactly (not just asymptotically).

Since OLS = MLE under normality (shown in Part 5), and \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \sigma^2/S_{xx}\) equals the CRLB:

\[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} = \text{CRLB for } \beta_1\]

An unbiased estimator that achieves the CRLB is, by definition, the MVUE.

These are parallel routes to the same destination. Lehmann-Scheffé works via the structure of the sufficient statistic; the CRLB route works via the information bound. Both require normality.

What BLUE Does Not Say

Common Misconceptions

BLUE does not mean:

  • OLS has the smallest variance among all estimators (biased or not): weighted least squares (WLS) or ridge regression can have smaller MSE
  • OLS is optimal when GM assumptions are violated: heteroscedasticity calls for WLS; dependence calls for generalized least squares (GLS)
  • The model is correct: OLS is BLUE for the stated model, regardless of whether the model fits the data well

BLUE does mean:

  • Within the class of linear, unbiased estimators, OLS cannot be beaten
  • This is a remarkable, assumption-lean result: no distributional assumption on \(\varepsilon_i\) beyond mean 0 and constant variance

Part 5: OLS = MLE Under Normality

The Normal Simple Linear Regression Model

Add one assumption to the GM setup:

\[\varepsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2)\]

This implies: \[Y_i \stackrel{\text{indep}}{\sim} N(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i, \; \sigma^2)\]

The joint likelihood is:

\[L(\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{(Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\]

\[= \left(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\right)^{n/2} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2\right\}\]

Maximizing the Likelihood

The log-likelihood is:

\[\ell(\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2\]

Key observation: For fixed \(\sigma^2\), maximizing \(\ell\) with respect to \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) is equivalent to minimizing:

\[\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2 = \text{RSS}\]

OLS = MLE Under Normality

The OLS estimators \(\hat\beta_0^{\text{OLS}}\) and \(\hat\beta_1^{\text{OLS}}\) are identical to the MLE estimators \(\hat\beta_0^{\text{MLE}}\) and \(\hat\beta_1^{\text{MLE}}\).

This is why we can use \(t\)-tests and \(F\)-tests in normal linear regression; the distributional framework that enables inference comes from normality + OLS = MLE.

From BLUE to MVUE: The Full Chain

Under the normal SLR model (\(\varepsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0,\sigma^2)\)):

Property Justification
\(\hat\beta_1\) is linear By construction: \(\hat\beta_1 = \sum c_i Y_i\)
\(\hat\beta_1\) is unbiased Shown above (only needs GM assumptions)
\(\hat\beta_1\) is BLUE Gauss-Markov Theorem (only needs GM assumptions)
\(\hat\beta_1\) is sufficient \((\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1, S^2)\) is sufficient for \((\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma^2)\) under normality
\(\hat\beta_1\) is the MLE OLS = MLE under normality (shown above)
\(\hat\beta_1\) is the MVUE Lehmann-Scheffé: complete sufficient statistic + unbiased
\(\hat\beta_1\) achieves CRLB MLE achieves CRLB asymptotically; exactly for exponential family

The Big Picture

This is precisely the situation we studied with scalar parameters: the MLE of an exponential family parameter based on the sufficient statistic is unbiased, complete, and achieves the CRLB. The normal SLR model is an exponential family model, and OLS produces the sufficient statistics.

Connecting to the CRLB

Under normal SLR, the Fisher information matrix for \((\beta_0, \beta_1)\) gives:

\[\text{CRLB for } \beta_1 = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} = \text{Var}(\hat\beta_1^{\text{OLS}})\]

\[\text{CRLB for } \beta_0 = \sigma^2\!\left(\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\right) = \text{Var}(\hat\beta_0^{\text{OLS}})\]

OLS estimators achieve the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound; they are efficient unbiased estimators.

Part 6: R Simulation: Verifying Gauss-Markov

Comparing OLS to Alternative Linear Estimators

The GM theorem says OLS beats every other linear unbiased estimator. Let’s verify with a simulation.

Setting: Same premature infant model: \(Y_i = -3200 + 170 x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 120^2)\)

Competitors:

  1. OLS: \(\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) (the Gauss-Markov champion)
  2. Naïve two-point slope: Use only the first and last observation: \(\tilde\beta_1^{(2)} = (Y_n - Y_1)/(x_n - x_1)\)
  3. Trimmed OLS: Drop middle third of observations, apply OLS to rest
  4. Unweighted mean of slopes: Average of all pairwise slopes (all pairs \(i < j\))

All four are linear in \(Y\) and unbiased, but only OLS is BLUE.

Code 
set.seed(551)
n_sim <- 5000
x_fixed <- sort(preterm$gestational_age)
n <- length(x_fixed)
beta0_true <- -3200; beta1_true <- 170; sigma <- 120

simulate_estimators <- function(x_vals, n_sim, beta0, beta1, sigma) {
  replicate(n_sim, {
    Y <- beta0 + beta1 * x_vals + rnorm(length(x_vals), 0, sigma)
    n  <- length(x_vals)
    
    # OLS slope
    ols <- coef(lm(Y ~ x_vals))[2]
    
    # Two-point slope: first and last observation
    two_pt <- (Y[n] - Y[1]) / (x_vals[n] - x_vals[1])
    
    # Trimmed OLS: drop middle third
    keep <- c(1:floor(n/3), ceiling(2*n/3):n)
    trimmed <- coef(lm(Y[keep] ~ x_vals[keep]))[2]
    
    # Mean of all pairwise slopes
    pairs_i <- rep(1:(n-1), times = (n-1):1)
    pairs_j <- unlist(lapply(2:n, \(j) j:n))
    pairwise_slopes <- (Y[pairs_j] - Y[pairs_i]) / (x_vals[pairs_j] - x_vals[pairs_i])
    pairwise_mean <- mean(pairwise_slopes)
    
    c(ols = as.numeric(ols), two_pt = two_pt, trimmed = as.numeric(trimmed), pairwise = pairwise_mean)
  }) |> t() |> as_tibble()
}

sim_results <- simulate_estimators(x_fixed, n_sim, beta0_true, beta1_true, sigma)

Part 7: Estimation of \(\sigma^2\) and Inference

Estimating the Error Variance \(\sigma^2\)

The GM assumptions include an unknown error variance \(\sigma^2\). We need to estimate it for inference.

Residuals: \(\hat\varepsilon_i = Y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i\)

Unbiased Estimator of \(\sigma^2\) (DBC §12.2)

\[\hat\sigma^2 = S^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n-2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i)^2}{n-2}\]

\(E[S^2] = \sigma^2\). Why \(n - 2\)? We lose 2 degrees of freedom because we estimate 2 parameters.

Under normality: \(\dfrac{(n-2)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-2}\), independent of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\).

This is the direct analog of how \(S^2\) worked in one-sample inference: the distribution changes because the number of estimated parameters changes.

Standard Errors and t-Based Inference

Replacing \(\sigma^2\) with \(S^2\) gives estimated standard errors:

\[\widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{S}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}} \qquad \widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat\beta_0) = S\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}}\]

Under normality, the pivot statistics follow \(t\) distributions:

\[T_1 = \frac{\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1}{S/\sqrt{S_{xx}}} \sim t_{n-2} \qquad T_0 = \frac{\hat\beta_0 - \beta_0}{S\sqrt{1/n + \bar x^2/S_{xx}}} \sim t_{n-2}\]

Part 8: When Gauss-Markov Fails

Violation: Heteroscedasticity

If \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2\) (not constant), OLS is still unbiased but is no longer BLUE.

Example: In a study of drug dosage and response, variance in response may increase with dose.

Code 
set.seed(42)
x_dose <- seq(1, 10, length.out = 80)
y_response <- 2 + 1.5 * x_dose + rnorm(80, 0, sd = 0.4 * x_dose)
hetero_data <- tibble(dose = x_dose, response = y_response)

ggplot(hetero_data, aes(x = dose, y = response)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6, color = "#2c3e50") +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, color = "#e74c3c") +
  labs(
    title = expression("Heteroscedasticity: " * Var(epsilon[i]) * " grows with dose"),
    x = "Drug Dose (mg/kg)", y = "Biomarker Response"
  )

Fix: Weighted Least Squares (WLS) minimizes \(\sum w_i (Y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2\) where \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i^2\), down-weighting observations with higher variance.

Weighted least squares is BLUE when weights are correctly specified. This is DBC §12.9.

Summary: The Hierarchy of OLS Optimality

Assumption Set OLS Property
GM 1–4 (linearity, independence, \(E[\varepsilon_i]=0\), homoscedasticity) BLUE: best among linear unbiased estimators
GM 1–4 + normality (\(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0,\sigma^2)\)) MVUE: best among all unbiased estimators
GM 1–4 + normality MLE: OLS and MLE coincide
GM 1–4 + normality Achieves CRLB: efficient
Homoscedasticity violated OLS unbiased, but not BLUE; use weighted least squares (WLS)
Independence violated OLS unbiased, but not BLUE; use generalized least squares (GLS)

The Takeaway for Applied Regression

When you check regression diagnostics (residual plots, Q-Q plots, leverage), you are verifying the GM assumptions that justify the optimality of OLS. If they fail, you need a different estimator.

Summary

What We Proved Today

Key Results

  1. OLS estimators \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar Y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x\) and \(\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) minimize the RSS

  2. OLS estimators are linear in \(Y_i\) and unbiased under GM assumptions 1–3

  3. Under all four GM assumptions: \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \sigma^2/S_{xx}\), \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) = \sigma^2(1/n + \bar x^2/S_{xx})\)

  4. Gauss-Markov Theorem: OLS is BLUE (no normality required)

  5. Under normality: OLS = MLE, and OLS is the MVUE (not just BLUE)

  6. OLS achieves the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound; it is an efficient estimator under normality

Connections to the Course Themes

Looking Ahead

  • Applied Regression: Everything builds on today’s theory
    • Multiple regression extends to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}\); the same BLUE result holds via matrix algebra
    • Generalized Linear Models (GLMs): when \(\varepsilon_i\) are non-normal → MLE replaces OLS
    • Mixed models: when independence fails → GLS / REML
  • Weighted Least Squares: BLUE when heteroscedasticity is known (DBC §12.9)

Matrix Formulation Preview

In multiple regression: \(\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\)

OLS estimate: \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}\)

This is still linear in \(\mathbf{Y}\), still unbiased under GM conditions, and still BLUE by the matrix version of the Gauss-Markov theorem.
