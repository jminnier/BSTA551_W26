CI for Mean: \(\sigma\) Unknown

The Realistic Case

In practice, we rarely know the population standard deviation \(\sigma\).

. . .

Natural idea: Replace \(\sigma\) with the sample standard deviation \(s\).

. . .

But what happens to our pivotal quantity?

\[Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0, 1)\]

becomes

\[T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim \; ???\]

The Source of Extra Variability

Key insight: T has more variability because both \(\bar{X}\) and \(S\) are random!

The t Distribution

The t distribution has heavier tails than the normal — accounting for extra uncertainty from estimating \(\sigma\).

Student’s t Distribution

Gosset’s Theorem

If \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), then: \[T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}\] where \(t_{n-1}\) denotes the t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

. . .

Key properties of \(t_\nu\):

  • Symmetric around 0
  • Heavier tails than \(N(0, 1)\)
  • As \(\nu \to \infty\), \(t_\nu \to N(0, 1)\)

Simulating the t Distribution (Chihara 7.1.2)

# Verify that (X̄ - μ)/(S/√n) follows t distribution
set.seed(551)
n <- 15
t_stats <- replicate(10000, {
  x <- rnorm(n, mean = 25, sd = 7)
  (mean(x) - 25) / (sd(x) / sqrt(n))
})

# Compare to theoretical t distribution
qqplot(qt(ppoints(10000), df = n - 1), t_stats, 
       main = "Q-Q Plot: Simulated vs. t(14)", 
       xlab = "Theoretical t(14)", ylab = "Simulated")
abline(0, 1, col = "red", lwd = 2)

Why \((n-1)\) Degrees of Freedom?

The sample variance involves: \[S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i - \bar{X})^2\]

. . .

Since \(\sum(X_i - \bar{X}) = 0\), knowing any \(n-1\) deviations determines the last one.

. . .

Intuition: We “use up” one degree of freedom by estimating \(\mu\) with \(\bar{X}\).

The One-Sample t Interval

t Confidence Interval for Normal Mean (\(\sigma\) Unknown)

If \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\mu\) is: \[\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\] where \(t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\) is the critical value with \(P(T > t_{\alpha/2, n-1}) = \alpha/2\) for \(T \sim t_{n-1}\).

. . .

The quantity \(s/\sqrt{n}\) is called the standard error of the mean (SEM or SE).

t Critical Values

When Can We Trust the t-Interval?

Guidelines for t-Interval Validity
  1. Large samples (n ≥ 30-40): t-interval is robust to non-normality (by CLT)
  2. Moderate samples (15-30): Check for severe skewness or outliers
  3. Small samples (n < 15): Normality is crucial; use Q-Q plots
  4. Outliers: Can severely affect \(\bar{x}\) and \(s\); consider alternatives

. . .

Alternatives for non-normal data:

  • Bootstrap confidence intervals (Lesson 9)
  • Non-parametric methods (median-based)
  • Transformations (log, square root)

Confidence Intervals for Proportions

The Setup

Situation: We observe \(X\) successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials.

  • \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)\)
  • Point estimate: \(\hat{p} = X/n\)

. . .

Examples in medical research:

  • Proportion of patients responding to treatment
  • Proportion experiencing adverse events
  • Disease prevalence in a population
  • Sensitivity/specificity of diagnostic tests

Deriving the MLE for Bernoulli (Review)

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p)\) where \(P(X_i = 1) = p\).

. . .

Likelihood function: \[L(p) = \prod_{i=1}^n p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i} = p^{\sum x_i}(1-p)^{n - \sum x_i}\]

. . .

Log-likelihood: \[\ell(p) = \left(\sum x_i\right) \ln(p) + \left(n - \sum x_i\right) \ln(1-p)\]

MLE for Bernoulli (continued)

Taking the derivative and setting to zero: \[\frac{d\ell}{dp} = \frac{\sum x_i}{p} - \frac{n - \sum x_i}{1-p} = 0\]

. . .

Solving for \(p\): \[\frac{\sum x_i}{p} = \frac{n - \sum x_i}{1-p}\] \[(1-p)\sum x_i = p(n - \sum x_i)\] \[\sum x_i = np\]

. . .

MLE for Bernoulli Parameter

\[\hat{p}_{MLE} = \frac{\sum X_i}{n} = \bar{X} = \frac{X}{n}\]

The General Wald Interval

Wald Confidence Interval (General Definition)

For an MLE \(\hat{\theta}\) with large \(n\), the Wald interval is based on the asymptotic normality of MLEs: \[\hat{\theta} \dot{\sim} N\left(\theta, \frac{1}{I_n(\theta)}\right)\]

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) Wald CI is: \[\hat{\theta} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \widehat{SE}(\hat{\theta})\] where \(\widehat{SE}\) is an estimate of the standard error, typically using \(\hat{\theta}\) in place of \(\theta\).

. . .

Key idea: Replace unknown parameters in the standard error with their MLEs.

Why the Wald Interval Works: Asymptotic Theory

From Lesson 4, we know the MLE has these asymptotic properties:

  1. Consistency: \(\hat{\theta} \xrightarrow{P} \theta\)

  2. Asymptotic normality: \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta} - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1/I(\theta))\)

. . .

For the Bernoulli case with \(\hat{p} = \bar{X}\):

  • \(E(\hat{p}) = p\) and \(\text{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\)
  • Fisher information: \(I(p) = \frac{1}{p(1-p)}\), so \(I_n(p) = \frac{n}{p(1-p)}\)
  • CRLB: \(\frac{1}{I_n(p)} = \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \text{Var}(\hat{p})\) ✓

Visualizing Convergence to N(0,1): Q-Q Plots

Code 
set.seed(551)
p_true <- 0.3
n_values <- c(10, 30, 100, 500)
n_sim <- 5000

# Simulate Wald pivot for various sample sizes
sim_wald_pivot <- function(n, p, n_sim) {
  replicate(n_sim, {
    x <- rbinom(1, n, p)
    p_hat <- x / n
    if (p_hat > 0 & p_hat < 1) {
      (p_hat - p) / sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
    } else {
      NA
    }
  })
}

# Generate data for all sample sizes
pivot_data <- map_dfr(n_values, ~{
  pivots <- sim_wald_pivot(.x, p_true, n_sim)
  pivots <- pivots[!is.na(pivots)]
  # Create Q-Q data: theoretical quantiles vs sample quantiles
  tibble(
    n = .x,
    n_label = paste0("n = ", .x),
    theoretical = qnorm(ppoints(length(pivots))),
    sample = sort(pivots)
  )
})

# Q-Q plots faceted by sample size
pivot_data |>
  mutate(n_label = factor(n_label, levels = paste0("n = ", n_values))) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = theoretical, y = sample)) +
  geom_abline(slope = 1, intercept = 0, color = "red", linewidth = 1.2, linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.3, size = 0.8, color = "steelblue") +
  facet_wrap(~n_label, nrow = 1) +
  coord_cartesian(xlim = c(-3.5, 3.5), ylim = c(-3.5, 3.5)) +
  labs(x = "Theoretical N(0,1) Quantiles",
       y = "Sample Quantiles of Wald Pivot",
       title = "Q-Q Plots: Wald Pivot Converges to N(0,1)",
       subtitle = "5,000 simulations per sample size | True p = 0.3 | Red dashed line = perfect N(0,1)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 16)

Points closer to the diagonal line indicate better agreement with N(0,1). Notice how the tails improve dramatically as \(n\) increases.

The Exact Pivot for Proportions

If we knew \(p\), we could use this exact pivot: \[Z = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)\]

. . .

Problem: The standard error \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) depends on the unknown \(p\)!

. . .

Wald solution: Replace \(p\) with \(\hat{p}\) in the standard error: \[Z^* = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}}\]

But is \(Z^*\) still approximately \(N(0,1)\)?

Why Plugging In \(\hat{p}\) Works: Slutsky’s Theorem

We can rewrite \(Z^*\) as: \[Z^* = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}} = \underbrace{\frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}}_{\xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}}}_{\xrightarrow{P} 1}\]

. . .

Slutsky’s Theorem: If \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) and \(Y_n \xrightarrow{P} c\) (a constant), then: \[X_n \cdot Y_n \xrightarrow{d} c \cdot X\]

. . .

Since \(\hat{p} \xrightarrow{P} p\) (consistency), we have: \[\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}} \xrightarrow{P} 1\]

Therefore: \(Z^* \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)\)

Visualizing the Consistency Argument

The Wald Interval for Proportions

Using \(Z^* = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}} \approx N(0,1)\) as our pivot:

\[P\left(-z_{\alpha/2} < \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}} < z_{\alpha/2}\right) \approx 1 - \alpha\]

. . .

Solving for \(p\):

Wald Confidence Interval for a Proportion

\[\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\]

. . .

Problems with the Wald Interval
  • Poor coverage probability for \(p\) near 0 or 1
  • Can produce bounds outside [0, 1]
  • Not recommended for small samples

Coverage Problems with Wald

The Wald interval often has coverage below the nominal level!

The Score Interval: A Better Approach

The Score Test Statistic (General Definition)

The score function is the derivative of the log-likelihood: \[U(\theta) = \ell'(\theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} \ln L(\theta)\]

The score test statistic is: \[Z_{score} = \frac{U(\theta)}{\sqrt{I_n(\theta)}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)\] where \(I_n(\theta)\) is the Fisher information.

. . .

Key insight: The score statistic is evaluated at the null/hypothesized parameter value, not at the MLE!

Score Function for Bernoulli

For \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p)\):

Log-likelihood: \(\ell(p) = X \ln(p) + (n-X)\ln(1-p)\) where \(X = \sum X_i\)

. . .

Score function: \[U(p) = \ell'(p) = \frac{X}{p} - \frac{n-X}{1-p} = \frac{X - np}{p(1-p)}\]

. . .

Fisher information: \(I_n(p) = \frac{n}{p(1-p)}\)

. . .

Score statistic: \[Z_{score} = \frac{U(p)}{\sqrt{I_n(p)}} = \frac{(X-np)/[p(1-p)]}{\sqrt{n/[p(1-p)]}} = \frac{X - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\]

Score Interval: Keep \(p\) in the Denominator!

Notice that the score statistic for proportions is: \[Z_{score} = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}} \approx N(0,1)\]

. . .

The score interval keeps \(p\) in the standard error (unlike Wald): \[\left|\frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\right| \leq z_{\alpha/2}\]

. . .

Squaring both sides: \[\frac{(\hat{p} - p)^2}{p(1-p)/n} \leq z_{\alpha/2}^2\]

. . .

This is a quadratic equation in \(p\)!

Solving the Quadratic for the Score Interval

Rearranging \((\hat{p} - p)^2 \leq z^2 \cdot p(1-p)/n\):

\[n(\hat{p} - p)^2 \leq z^2 p(1-p)\]

\[n\hat{p}^2 - 2np\hat{p} + np^2 \leq z^2 p - z^2 p^2\]

\[(n + z^2)p^2 - (2n\hat{p} + z^2)p + n\hat{p}^2 \leq 0\]

. . .

Solving with the quadratic formula gives the Score (Wilson) interval:

Score Confidence Interval for a Proportion

\[\frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}\] where \(z = z_{\alpha/2}\).

Why Score Intervals Are Better

Score interval maintains coverage closer to nominal level, especially for extreme \(p\).

The Agresti-Coull Interval

A simpler approximation that works well in practice:

Agresti-Coull (“Add Two Successes and Two Failures”)

Let \(\tilde{n} = n + 4\) and \(\tilde{p} = \frac{X + 2}{n + 4}\)

Then use: \[\tilde{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{p}(1-\tilde{p})}{\tilde{n}}}\]

. . .

Why it works: Adding 2 successes and 2 failures “shrinks” extreme estimates toward 0.5, improving coverage.

. . .

Connection to Score: With \(z_{0.025} \approx 2\), the Score interval is approximately Agresti-Coull!

Comparing All Three Methods

compare_proportion_cis <- function(x, n, conf = 0.95) {
  p_hat <- x/n
  z <- qnorm(1 - (1-conf)/2)
  
  # Wald
  se_w <- sqrt(p_hat * (1-p_hat) / n)
  wald <- p_hat + c(-1, 1) * z * se_w
  
  # Agresti-Coull
  p_ac <- (x + 2) / (n + 4)
  se_ac <- sqrt(p_ac * (1-p_ac) / (n + 4))
  agresti <- p_ac + c(-1, 1) * z * se_ac
  
  # Score (Wilson)
  score <- prop.test(x, n, conf.level = conf, correct = FALSE)$conf.int
  
  tibble(Method = c("Wald", "Agresti-Coull", "Score"),
         Lower = c(wald[1], agresti[1], score[1]),
         Upper = c(wald[2], agresti[2], score[2]))
}

compare_proportion_cis(23, 40)

Extreme Proportions: Why Wald Fails

# What if only 2 of 50 patients have adverse events?
x <- 2; n <- 50; p_hat <- x/n

# Wald can give negative lower bound!
se_wald <- sqrt(p_hat * (1-p_hat) / n)
wald_ci <- p_hat + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * se_wald

cat("Wald 95% CI:  (", round(wald_ci[1], 4), ",", round(wald_ci[2], 4), ")\n")
Wald 95% CI:  ( -0.0143 , 0.0943 )
# Score interval stays in [0,1]
score_ci <- prop.test(x, n, conf.level = 0.95, correct = FALSE)$conf.int
cat("Score 95% CI: (", round(score_ci[1], 4), ",", round(score_ci[2], 4), ")")
Score 95% CI: ( 0.011 , 0.1346 )

The Wald interval gives a negative lower bound — impossible for a proportion!

Sample Size for Proportions

To achieve a margin of error \(b\) with confidence level \(1 - \alpha\):

\[n = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \cdot p(1-p)}{b^2}\]

. . .

Problem: We don’t know \(p\) before the study!

. . .

Conservative approach: Use \(p = 0.5\) (maximizes \(p(1-p) = 0.25\)): \[n = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \cdot 0.25}{b^2}\]

Sample Size Example: Disease Prevalence

An epidemiologist wants to estimate disease prevalence with a margin of error ≤ 3% with 95% confidence.

b <- 0.03  # margin of error
z <- qnorm(0.975)

# Conservative (p = 0.5)
n_conservative <- z^2 * 0.25 / b^2
cat("Conservative sample size:", ceiling(n_conservative), "\n")
Conservative sample size: 1068 
# If we believe p ≈ 0.10 from prior studies
n_informed <- z^2 * 0.10 * 0.90 / b^2
cat("Informed sample size (p ≈ 0.10):", ceiling(n_informed))
Informed sample size (p ≈ 0.10): 385

. . .

Using prior information can substantially reduce required sample size!

Practical Considerations

Summary: CI Methods Comparison

Method When to Use Notes
Z-interval Normal data, \(\sigma\) known Rarely applicable
t-interval Normal data, \(\sigma\) unknown Check normality
Wald (proportion) Large \(n\), \(p\) not extreme Poor coverage
Score/Wilson General Best for proportions
Agresti-Coull Quick calculation Good approximation

CI Interpretation in Medical Research

Valid Interpretations
  • “We are 95% confident that \(\mu\) lies between L and U”
  • “The interval (L, U) is a plausible range for \(\mu\)”
  • “In repeated sampling, 95% of such intervals would contain \(\mu\)”

. . .

Invalid Interpretations
  • “There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in (L, U)” ✗
  • “95% of the data falls between L and U” ✗
  • “The true mean varies between L and U” ✗

Clinical Decision Making with CIs

Example: A drug reduces blood pressure by an estimated 12 mmHg with 95% CI (8, 16).

. . .

Questions we can answer:

  • Is the effect clinically meaningful? (If minimum important difference is 5 mmHg, yes!)
  • Is zero a plausible value? (No — CI excludes 0)
  • What’s the worst-case scenario? (Lower bound suggests at least 8 mmHg effect)

Relationship to Hypothesis Testing

Preview of Coming Material

A 95% CI contains all parameter values that would not be rejected by a two-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

  • If CI excludes the null value → reject \(H_0\)
  • If CI includes the null value → fail to reject \(H_0\)

. . .

Example: 95% CI for mean difference is (8, 16).

  • Test \(H_0: \mu = 0\) → Reject (0 not in CI)
  • Test \(H_0: \mu = 10\) → Fail to reject (10 in CI)

Lesson 8 Summary

Key Concepts:

  1. t-intervals handle unknown \(\sigma\): \(\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)

  2. t distribution has heavier tails than normal; approaches normal as df → ∞

  3. Always check normality for t-intervals (Q-Q plots, histograms)

  4. Wald intervals based on asymptotic MLE theory; plug in \(\hat{\theta}\) for SE

  5. Score intervals keep the parameter in SE, solve quadratic; better coverage

  6. Agresti-Coull (“add 2 and 2”) is a good simple approximation

Key Formulas

Parameter CI Formula Conditions
\(\mu\) (\(\sigma\) known) \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) Normal data
\(\mu\) (\(\sigma\) unknown) \(\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\) Normal data
\(p\) (Wald) \(\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\) Large \(n\)
\(p\) (Agresti-Coull) \(\tilde{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{p}(1-\tilde{p})}{\tilde{n}}}\) General
