Lesson 3: Finding the Best Estimator

Review: Where We Left Off

Key concepts from Lesson 2:

  • Point estimators vs. estimates
  • Bias: \(\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta\)
  • Unbiasedness: \(E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta\)
  • MSE = Variance + Bias²

. . .

Today’s Goals:

  • Understand what makes one unbiased estimator better than another
  • Learn about Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimators (MVUE)
  • Introduce the Method of Moments for constructing estimators
  • Apply these methods to medical research examples

Motivating Example: Estimating Drug Efficacy

A clinical trial tests the effectiveness of a new antibiotic. Two researchers propose different estimators for the true response rate \(p\):

. . .

Researcher A: Use \(\hat{p}_A = X/n\) (sample proportion)

Researcher B: Use \(\hat{p}_B = (X + 1)/(n + 2)\) (adjusted proportion)

. . .

Both estimators are functions of the same data. Which should we use?

# Suppose 15 out of 20 patients respond
n <- 20; x <- 15
p_hat_A <- x / n
p_hat_B <- (x + 1) / (n + 2)
cat("Researcher A's estimate:", p_hat_A, "\n")
Researcher A's estimate: 0.75 
cat("Researcher B's estimate:", p_hat_B)
Researcher B's estimate: 0.7272727

Choosing Among Unbiased Estimators

The Problem of Multiple Unbiased Estimators

Recall from Lesson 2: There can be many unbiased estimators for the same parameter!

. . .

Example: For a symmetric distribution with mean \(\mu\):

  • Sample mean \(\bar{X}\) is unbiased for \(\mu\)
  • Sample median \(\tilde{X}\) is unbiased for \(\mu\)
  • Trimmed means are unbiased for \(\mu\)

. . .

The Key Question

If two estimators are both unbiased, how do we choose between them?

Comparing Two Unbiased Estimators: Intuition

Answer: Among unbiased estimators, prefer the one with smaller variance.

The Principle: Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimator

Definition (Devore 7.1)

The Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimator (MVUE) of a parameter \(\theta\) is the estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) such that:

  1. \(\hat{\theta}\) is unbiased: \(E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta\)
  2. \(\hat{\theta}\) has smaller or equal variance than any other unbiased estimator

If \(\hat{\theta}_1\) and \(\hat{\theta}_2\) are both unbiased for \(\theta\), we prefer the one with smaller variance.

. . .

Intuition: Among estimators that are “correct on average,” we want the most precise one.

Example: Uniform Distribution Upper Bound

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim \text{Uniform}[0, \theta]\)

Two unbiased estimators for \(\theta\):

. . .

  1. \(\hat{\theta}_1 = 2\bar{X}\) (since \(E(X_i) = \theta/2\), so \(E(2\bar{X}) = \theta\))

  2. \(\hat{\theta}_2 = \frac{n+1}{n} \cdot \max(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) (corrected maximum)

. . .

Question: Which has smaller variance?

Calculating Variances

For \(\hat{\theta}_1 = 2\bar{X}\): \[\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_1) = \text{Var}(2\bar{X}) = 4 \cdot \text{Var}(\bar{X}) = 4 \cdot \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = 4 \cdot \frac{\theta^2/12}{n} = \frac{\theta^2}{3n}\]

. . .

For \(\hat{\theta}_2 = \frac{n+1}{n} \cdot \max(X_i)\), it can be shown that: \[\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_2) = \frac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}\]

. . .

Comparison: For \(n > 1\): \[\frac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)} < \frac{\theta^2}{3n}\]

The corrected maximum \(\hat{\theta}_2\) is more efficient!

Simulation: Comparing the Two Estimators

theta <- 10  # True upper bound
n <- 20
n_sims <- 5000

# Simulate both estimators
uniform_sim <- tibble(sim = 1:n_sims) |> 
  mutate(
    data = map(sim, \(s) runif(n, 0, theta)),
    theta_hat_1 = map_dbl(data, \(d) 2 * mean(d)),
    theta_hat_2 = map_dbl(data, \(d) (n + 1) / n * max(d))
  )

# Compare variances
uniform_sim |> 
  summarize(
    `Var(2*X̄)` = var(theta_hat_1),
    `Var(corrected max)` = var(theta_hat_2),
    `Theoretical Var(2*X̄)` = theta^2 / (3 * n),
    `Theoretical Var(corrected max)` = theta^2 / (n * (n + 2))
  ) |> 
  pivot_longer(everything()) |> 
  mutate(value = round(value, 4))
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  name                           value
  <chr>                          <dbl>
1 Var(2*X̄)                       1.67 
2 Var(corrected max)             0.218
3 Theoretical Var(2*X̄)           1.67 
4 Theoretical Var(corrected max) 0.227

Visualizing the Comparison

The corrected maximum is more concentrated around the true value!

The MVUE Theorem for Normal Distributions

Theorem (Devore 7.1)

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be a random sample from a normal distribution with parameters \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). Then:

The sample mean \(\bar{X}\) is the MVUE for \(\mu\).

. . .

Why this matters: When sampling from a normal population, no other unbiased estimator of \(\mu\) can have smaller variance than \(\bar{X}\).

. . .

This is a powerful result! It tells us that \(\bar{X}\) is optimal (in the MVUE sense) for normal data.

Medical Application: Blood Pressure Reduction

A pharmaceutical trial measures systolic blood pressure reduction (mmHg) in patients taking a new medication. Assume reductions are normally distributed.

# Blood pressure reductions from 30 patients
bp_data <- c(12.3, 8.7, 15.2, 10.1, 7.4, 14.8, 11.2, 9.5, 13.1, 16.2,
             8.1, 12.5, 10.8, 14.3, 11.7, 9.2, 15.0, 13.4, 7.9, 12.1,
             10.5, 8.3, 14.1, 11.5, 13.8, 9.8, 11.9, 12.7, 10.3, 14.6)

# MVUE for mean reduction
x_bar <- mean(bp_data)
se_x_bar <- sd(bp_data) / sqrt(length(bp_data))

cat("MVUE of mean reduction:", round(x_bar, 2), "mmHg\n")
MVUE of mean reduction: 11.7 mmHg
cat("Standard error:", round(se_x_bar, 2), "mmHg")
Standard error: 0.45 mmHg

Since the data is assumed normal, \(\bar{X}\) is not just unbiased—it’s the best unbiased estimator.

Your Turn: Comparing Estimators

Exercise: For a normal distribution with known \(\sigma = 10\), which estimator of \(\mu\) has smaller variance?

  • \(\hat{\mu}_1 = \bar{X}\) (sample mean)
  • \(\hat{\mu}_2 = \tilde{X}\) (sample median)

For a normal distribution with \(n = 20\):

  • \(\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2/n = 100/20 = 5\)
  • \(\text{Var}(\tilde{X}) \approx \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \approx 1.57 \cdot 5 = 7.85\)

. . .

The sample mean wins! For normal data, \(\bar{X}\) is the MVUE.

When Might the Median Be Better?

Although \(\bar{X}\) is MVUE for normal data, the median can be better for:

  • Heavy-tailed distributions (where outliers are more common)
  • Contaminated data (mixture of two distributions)

. . .

Method of Moments

Motivation: How Do We Find Estimators?

So far, we’ve evaluated estimators. But how do we construct them in the first place?

. . .

Two major constructive methods:

  1. Method of Moments (today)
  2. Maximum Likelihood (Lesson 4)

. . .

Key Idea

Both methods give us systematic recipes for deriving estimators from any specified distribution.

Population vs. Sample Moments

Definitions (Devore 7.2)

Population Moments:

  • 1st moment: \(E(X) = \mu\)
  • 2nd moment: \(E(X^2)\)
  • kth moment: \(E(X^k)\)

Sample Moments:

  • 1st sample moment: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i = \bar{X}\)
  • 2nd sample moment: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2\)
  • kth sample moment: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^k\)

The Method of Moments Principle

Method of Moments Estimators (MMEs)

To estimate \(m\) unknown parameters \(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_m\):

  1. Express the first \(m\) population moments as functions of the parameters
  2. Set each population moment equal to its sample counterpart
  3. Solve the resulting system of equations for \(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_m\)

. . .

The idea: If the population and sample moments should be “close,” we can estimate parameters by equating them.

Example 1: Exponential Distribution (One Parameter)

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\) where \(\lambda\) is the rate parameter.

Goal: Find the MME for \(\lambda\).

. . .

Step 1: Population moment: \(E(X) = 1/\lambda\)

Step 2: Set equal to sample moment: \(\frac{1}{\lambda} = \bar{X}\)

Step 3: Solve: \(\hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\bar{X}}\)

. . .

Intuition

If the population mean is \(1/\lambda\), and we estimate the population mean with \(\bar{X}\), then we estimate \(\lambda\) with \(1/\bar{X}\).

Medical Application: Hospital Wait Times

Emergency room wait times are often modeled with an exponential distribution. The parameter \(\lambda\) represents the rate (patients served per hour).

# Wait times (minutes) for 40 patients
wait_times <- c(12, 45, 8, 23, 67, 15, 31, 42, 19, 56,
                28, 11, 38, 22, 49, 17, 33, 25, 41, 14,
                52, 9, 36, 27, 44, 18, 29, 47, 21, 35,
                13, 39, 24, 51, 16, 32, 46, 20, 37, 26)

# Method of moments estimate for λ (per minute)
lambda_hat <- 1 / mean(wait_times)

cat("Sample mean wait time:", round(mean(wait_times), 2), "minutes\n")
Sample mean wait time: 30.45 minutes
cat("MME of λ:", round(lambda_hat, 4), "per minute\n")
MME of λ: 0.0328 per minute
cat("MME of λ:", round(lambda_hat * 60, 2), "per hour")
MME of λ: 1.97 per hour

Verifying with Simulation

# Compare empirical distribution to fitted exponential
tibble(wait_time = wait_times) |> 
  ggplot(aes(x = wait_time)) +
  geom_histogram(aes(y = after_stat(density)), bins = 15, 
                 fill = "steelblue", alpha = 0.7) +
  stat_function(fun = dexp, args = list(rate = lambda_hat),
                color = "red", linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(title = "ER Wait Times with Fitted Exponential Distribution",
       subtitle = str_glue("MME: λ̂ = {round(lambda_hat, 4)}"),

  annotate("text", x = 50, y = 0.025, 
           label = str_glue("Mean = {round(mean(wait_times), 1)} min"),
           color = "red", size = 5)

Example 2: Normal Distribution (Two Parameters)

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\)

Goal: Find MMEs for \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).

. . .

Population moments:

  • \(E(X) = \mu\)
  • \(E(X^2) = \mu^2 + \sigma^2\) (variance shortcut formula!)

. . .

Set equal to sample moments:

  • \(\mu = \bar{X}\)
  • \(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2\)

Solving for the Normal MMEs

From the two equations:

\[\hat{\mu} = \bar{X}\]

. . .

\[\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 - \bar{X}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2\]

. . .

Note on Bias

The MME for \(\sigma^2\) divides by \(n\), not \(n-1\). This means the MME is biased!

\[E(\hat{\sigma}^2_{MME}) = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\]

The usual sample variance \(S^2\) with \(n-1\) in the denominator is unbiased.

Example 3: Gamma Distribution (Two Parameters)

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\)

Moments:

  • \(E(X) = \alpha\beta\)
  • \(E(X^2) = \alpha(\alpha+1)\beta^2\)

. . .

MME equations: \[\bar{X} = \alpha\beta\] \[\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 = \alpha(\alpha+1)\beta^2\]

. . .

Solutions (after algebra): \[\hat{\alpha} = \frac{\bar{X}^2}{\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 - \bar{X}^2} \quad \quad \hat{\beta} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 - \bar{X}^2}{\bar{X}}\]

Medical Application: Surgery Recovery Times

Recovery times after a particular surgery often follow a gamma distribution. Let’s estimate the parameters.

# Recovery times (days) for 25 patients
recovery_times <- c(4.2, 6.8, 5.1, 8.3, 7.2, 4.9, 6.1, 9.5, 5.7, 7.8,
                    6.4, 8.1, 5.3, 7.5, 6.9, 4.5, 8.7, 5.9, 7.1, 6.2,
                    9.2, 5.5, 7.4, 6.6, 8.4)

# Calculate sample moments
x_bar <- mean(recovery_times)
second_moment <- mean(recovery_times^2)

# MME for gamma parameters
alpha_hat <- x_bar^2 / (second_moment - x_bar^2)
beta_hat <- (second_moment - x_bar^2) / x_bar

cat("Sample mean:", round(x_bar, 2), "days\n")
Sample mean: 6.77 days
cat("MME of α:", round(alpha_hat, 2), "\n")
MME of α: 22.56 
cat("MME of β:", round(beta_hat, 2), "days")
MME of β: 0.3 days

Visualizing the Gamma Fit

tibble(recovery_time = recovery_times) |> 
  ggplot(aes(x = recovery_time)) +
  geom_histogram(aes(y = after_stat(density)), bins = 10, 
                 fill = "steelblue", alpha = 0.7) +
  stat_function(fun = dgamma, 
                args = list(shape = alpha_hat, scale = beta_hat),
                color = "red", linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(title = "Surgery Recovery Times with Fitted Gamma Distribution",
       subtitle = str_glue("MME: α̂ = {round(alpha_hat, 2)}, β̂ = {round(beta_hat, 2)}"),
       x = "Recovery Time (days)", y = "Density")

Your Turn: Method of Moments

Exercise: Drug concentrations in blood plasma follow a Uniform[0, \(\theta\)] distribution. From \(n = 15\) measurements, you observe \(\bar{x} = 4.2\) mg/L.

Find the MME of \(\theta\).

. . .

Solution:

For Uniform[0, \(\theta\)]: \(E(X) = \theta/2\)

Setting equal to sample mean: \(\bar{x} = \theta/2\)

Therefore: \(\hat{\theta} = 2\bar{x} = 2(4.2) = 8.4\) mg/L

Properties of Method of Moments Estimators

Advantages:

  • Simple to compute (usually just algebra)
  • Often has closed-form solutions
  • Usually consistent (converges to true value as \(n \to \infty\))

. . .

Disadvantages:

  • May not be the most efficient (highest variance)
  • Can sometimes give impossible values
  • May be biased

. . .

Coming Up

Maximum likelihood estimators (Lesson 4) generally have better efficiency properties but require more computation.

Example: When MME Gives Impossible Values

Problem: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim \text{Uniform}[0, \beta]\)

The MME is \(\hat{\beta} = 2\bar{X}\).

. . .

Potential issue: What if some \(X_i > 2\bar{X}\)?

# Example data where MME fails
problematic_data <- c(1, 1, 1, 9)  # max = 9
beta_mme <- 2 * mean(problematic_data)  # = 6

cat("Data:", problematic_data, "\n")
Data: 1 1 1 9 
cat("MME of β:", beta_mme, "\n")
MME of β: 6 
cat("But max observation is:", max(problematic_data), "\n")
But max observation is: 9 
cat("This is impossible if β =", beta_mme, "!")
This is impossible if β = 6 !

The MME suggests \(\beta = 6\), but we observed a value of 9!

Practical Considerations

Computing MMEs in R: A Workflow

# General workflow for method of moments
# Step 1: Collect data
data <- c(2.3, 4.1, 3.8, 5.2, 2.9, 4.5, 3.1, 4.8, 3.5, 4.2)

# Step 2: Compute sample moments
compute_sample_moments <- function(x, k = 2) {
  moments <- map_dbl(1:k, \(j) mean(x^j))
  names(moments) <- paste0("m", 1:k)
  moments
}

sample_moments <- compute_sample_moments(data, k = 2)
cat("First moment (mean):", round(sample_moments["m1"], 3), "\n")
First moment (mean): 3.84 
cat("Second moment:", round(sample_moments["m2"], 3), "\n")
Second moment: 15.478 
# Step 3: Solve for parameters (depends on distribution)
# Example: Normal distribution
mu_hat <- sample_moments["m1"]
sigma2_hat <- sample_moments["m2"] - sample_moments["m1"]^2
cat("\nNormal MMEs:\n")

Normal MMEs:
cat("μ̂ =", round(mu_hat, 3), "\n")
μ̂ = 3.84 
cat("σ̂² =", round(sigma2_hat, 3))
σ̂² = 0.732

Medical Research Application: Drug Clearance

Drug clearance from the body often follows an exponential decay model. A pharmacokinetics study measures the half-life of a new medication.

# Half-lives (hours) from 35 patients
half_lives <- c(3.2, 4.1, 2.8, 3.5, 4.8, 3.1, 3.9, 2.6, 4.3, 3.7,
                3.4, 4.0, 2.9, 3.8, 4.5, 3.3, 3.6, 4.2, 3.0, 3.9,
                2.7, 4.1, 3.5, 3.8, 4.0, 3.2, 3.7, 4.4, 3.1, 3.6,
                2.8, 4.0, 3.4, 3.9, 3.5)

# Assume gamma distribution; compute MMEs
x_bar <- mean(half_lives)
var_x <- var(half_lives) * (length(half_lives) - 1) / length(half_lives)

alpha_mme <- x_bar^2 / var_x
beta_mme <- var_x / x_bar

cat("Sample statistics:\n")
Sample statistics:
cat("  Mean half-life:", round(x_bar, 2), "hours\n")
  Mean half-life: 3.61 hours
cat("  Sample variance:", round(var_x, 3), "\n\n")
  Sample variance: 0.287 
cat("Gamma MMEs:\n")
Gamma MMEs:
cat("  α̂ =", round(alpha_mme, 2), "\n")
  α̂ = 45.36

  β̂ = 0.08 hours

Connecting Moments to Variance

Recall the variance formula: \(\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2\)

. . .

This connects moments to variance!

\[\sigma^2 = \mu_2' - (\mu_1')^2\]

where \(\mu_k' = E(X^k)\) is the kth raw moment.

. . .

For sample moments: \[\hat{\sigma}^2_{MME} = \frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 - \bar{X}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2\]

This is why the MME for variance divides by \(n\), not \(n-1\).

Summary Table: Common MMEs

Distribution Parameters MME Formula
Normal \(\mu\), \(\sigma^2\) \(\hat{\mu} = \bar{X}\), \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i-\bar{X})^2\)
Exponential \(\lambda\) \(\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{X}\)
Gamma \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{X}^2/\hat{\sigma}^2\), \(\hat{\beta} = \hat{\sigma}^2/\bar{X}\)
Uniform[0,\(\theta\)] \(\theta\) \(\hat{\theta} = 2\bar{X}\)
Poisson \(\lambda\) \(\hat{\lambda} = \bar{X}\)

Summary and Looking Ahead

Lesson 3 Summary

Key Concepts:

  1. MVUE: Among unbiased estimators, prefer the one with smallest variance
    • \(\bar{X}\) is MVUE for \(\mu\) in normal distributions
    • The corrected maximum is MVUE for uniform upper bound
  2. Method of Moments:
    • Equate population moments to sample moments
    • Solve for unknown parameters
    • Simple but may not be most efficient
  3. Moments:
    • kth population moment: \(E(X^k)\)
    • kth sample moment: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^k\)
