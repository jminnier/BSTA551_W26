Frequentist vs. Bayesian Inference

Two Paradigms for Statistical Inference

Frequentist (Classical) Inference:

Observe data \(X \sim f(x|\theta)\) \(\longrightarrow\) use observed data \(x\) to make statements about \(\theta\), which is assumed to be a fixed (and unknown) model parameter.

  • Point estimates, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests
  • \(\theta\) is a constant; probability statements are about the data, not \(\theta\)
  • Confidence interval interpretation: long-run frequency of coverage

Bayesian Inference:

Model random variable \(\theta \sim \pi(\theta)\) \(\longrightarrow\) observe \(X|\theta \sim f(x|\theta)\) \(\longrightarrow\) update with \(\pi(\theta|x)\)

  • \(\theta\) is treated as a random variable with a probability distribution
  • Prior distribution \(\pi(\theta)\): incorporates available information about \(\theta\) before data are observed
  • Posterior distribution \(\pi(\theta|x)\): “updated” distribution of \(\theta\) after the data are observed
  • All inference comes from the posterior

Why Consider Bayesian Methods?

Advantages:

  • Incorporates prior knowledge or expert opinion formally
  • Posterior probability statements are direct: \(P(\theta \in A | x)\) has a natural interpretation
  • Credible intervals say “there is a 95% probability that \(\theta\) is in this interval”
  • Naturally handles nuisance parameters through marginalization
  • Provides a coherent framework for sequential updating of evidence

Challenges:

  • Requires specifying a prior distribution (can be subjective)
  • Choice of prior can strongly influence results, especially with small samples
  • Computational demands can be substantial for complex models
  • Philosophical objections to treating fixed quantities as random

Medical research context: Bayesian methods are increasingly used in clinical trials (adaptive designs), diagnostic testing, meta-analysis, and regulatory submissions (e.g., medical device trials at the FDA).

Prior and Posterior Distributions

Prior Distributions

Definition: Prior Distribution (DBC 15.1)

A prior distribution for a parameter \(\theta\), denoted \(\pi(\theta)\), is a probability distribution on the set of possible values for \(\theta\). If \(\theta\) takes values in an interval \(I\), then \(\pi(\theta)\) is a pdf satisfying \(\int_I \pi(\theta) \, d\theta = 1\). If \(\theta\) takes values in a discrete set \(D\), then \(\pi(\theta)\) is a pmf satisfying \(\sum_{\theta \in D} \pi(\theta) = 1\).

The variability of the prior distribution reflects how certain we are about our initial belief:

  • A narrow (concentrated) prior expresses strong belief
  • A wide (diffuse) prior expresses substantial uncertainty
  • A flat or noninformative prior attempts to let the data speak for themselves
Code 
# Prior distributions with different levels of certainty
tibble(theta = seq(0, 10, by = 0.01)) |>
  mutate(
    `Strong belief (narrow)` = dnorm(theta, mean = 5, sd = 0.5),
    `Moderate belief` = dnorm(theta, mean = 5, sd = 1.5),
    `Weak belief (diffuse)` = dnorm(theta, mean = 5, sd = 4)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(-theta, names_to = "Prior", values_to = "density") |>
  ggplot(aes(x = theta, y = density, color = Prior, linetype = Prior)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(
    x = expression(theta),
    y = expression(pi(theta)),
    title = "Prior distributions reflecting different levels of certainty"
  ) +
  scale_color_brewer(palette = "Set2") +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

The Posterior Distribution

The key to Bayesian inference is Bayes’ theorem applied to random variables.

Definition: Posterior Distribution

Suppose \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) have joint pdf \(f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta)\) and \(\theta\) has been assigned a prior distribution \(\pi(\theta)\). The posterior distribution of \(\theta\) given the observed data is:

\[\pi(\theta | x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \frac{\pi(\theta) \, f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta)}{\int \pi(\theta) \, f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta) \, d\theta}\]

The denominator \(m(x) = \int \pi(\theta) f(x|\theta) \, d\theta\) is the marginal distribution of the data. Since it does not depend on \(\theta\), we often write:

\[\pi(\theta | x) \propto \underbrace{\pi(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} \times \underbrace{f(x|\theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\]

\[\boxed{\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Prior} \times \text{Likelihood}}\]

This is the fundamental equation of Bayesian inference.

Bayesian Updating: The Full Picture

The complete set of distributions in a Bayesian analysis:

  1. Prior distribution: \(\theta \sim \pi(\theta)\) (set in advance)
  2. Data model (likelihood): \(X|\theta \sim f(x|\theta)\) (set in advance)
  3. Joint distribution: \(f_{X,\theta}(x, \theta) = f(x|\theta) \, \pi(\theta)\)
  4. Marginal distribution: \(m(x) = \int f(x|\theta) \, \pi(\theta) \, d\theta\)
  5. Posterior distribution: \(\pi(\theta|x) = \frac{f(x|\theta) \, \pi(\theta)}{m(x)}\)

The posterior combines prior belief with observed evidence from the data. As we collect more data, the posterior becomes increasingly driven by the likelihood.

Conjugate Priors

What Are Conjugate Priors?

In practice, we want to choose a prior distribution family such that the posterior distribution belongs to the same family. When this happens, we say the prior is conjugate to the data distribution.

Definition: Conjugate Prior

A prior distribution \(\pi(\theta)\) from family \(\mathcal{F}\) is conjugate to the data distribution \(f(x|\theta)\) if the posterior distribution \(\pi(\theta|x)\) also belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\).

Why conjugate priors?

  • Posterior has a closed-form expression — no numerical integration needed
  • The prior hyperparameters have interpretable roles (e.g., “prior sample size”)
  • Easy to update sequentially as new data arrive

Common conjugate pairs:

Data Model Conjugate Prior Posterior
Bernoulli/Binomial(\(p\)) Beta(\(\alpha_0, \beta_0\)) Beta(\(\alpha_0 + y, \beta_0 + n - y\))
Poisson(\(\lambda\)) Gamma(\(\alpha_0, \beta_0\)) Gamma(\(\alpha_0 + \sum x_i, \frac{1}{n + 1/\beta_0}\))
Exponential(\(\lambda\)) Gamma(\(\alpha_0, \beta_0\)) Gamma(\(\alpha_0 + n, \frac{1}{1/\beta_0 + \sum x_i}\))
Normal(\(\mu\), \(\sigma^2\) known) Normal(\(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2\)) Normal(\(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2\))

Example: Beta Prior for Binomial Data (DBC 15.3, C&H 10.3)

Proposition: Beta-Binomial Conjugacy

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be a random sample from a Bernoulli(\(p\)) distribution. Equivalently, let \(Y = \sum X_i \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)\). If \(p\) is assigned a Beta(\(\alpha_0, \beta_0\)) prior, then the posterior distribution of \(p\) given the data is:

\[p \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + y, \;\beta_0 + n - y)\]

Proof: The joint Bernoulli pmf is \(f(x|p) = p^y(1-p)^{n-y}\) where \(y = \sum x_i\). The Beta prior is \(\pi(p) \propto p^{\alpha_0 - 1}(1-p)^{\beta_0 - 1}\). Applying Bayes’ theorem:

\[\pi(p|x) \propto p^{\alpha_0 - 1}(1-p)^{\beta_0 - 1} \cdot p^y(1-p)^{n-y} = p^{(\alpha_0 + y) - 1}(1-p)^{(\beta_0 + n - y) - 1}\]

We recognize this as proportional to the Beta(\(\alpha_0 + y, \beta_0 + n - y\)) density. \(\square\)

Interpretation: The hyperparameters \(\alpha_0\) and \(\beta_0\) act like “prior successes” and “prior failures.” The posterior adds observed successes \(y\) and observed failures \(n - y\).

Medical Example: Beta-Binomial — Vaccine Efficacy

A Phase II clinical trial tests a new vaccine. Prior studies suggest efficacy around 60%, but with substantial uncertainty. We use a Beta(6, 4) prior for \(p\) (the true efficacy rate), which has mean \(6/10 = 0.60\).

In the trial, \(n = 50\) patients are vaccinated and \(y = 38\) show a positive immune response.

Code 
# Beta-Binomial: Vaccine efficacy
a0 <- 6; b0 <- 4       # Prior hyperparameters
n <- 50; y <- 38         # Observed data

# Posterior parameters
a_post <- a0 + y         # 44
b_post <- b0 + n - y     # 16

tibble(p = seq(0, 1, by = 0.001)) |>
  mutate(
    Prior = dbeta(p, a0, b0),
    Likelihood = dbinom(y, n, p) * 150,  # scaled for visualization
    Posterior = dbeta(p, a_post, b_post)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(-p, names_to = "Distribution", values_to = "density") |>
  mutate(Distribution = factor(Distribution, levels = c("Prior", "Likelihood", "Posterior"))) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = density, color = Distribution, linetype = Distribution)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(
    x = expression(p),
    y = "Density",
    title = "Bayesian updating: Vaccine efficacy",
    subtitle = paste0(
      "Prior: Beta(", a0, ", ", b0, ") | Data: ", y, "/", n,
      " | Posterior: Beta(", a_post, ", ", b_post, ")"
    )
  ) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("Prior" = "steelblue", "Likelihood" = "gray50", "Posterior" = "darkred")) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")
  • Prior mean: \(\frac{6}{10} = 0.60\)
  • MLE (frequentist): \(\hat{p} = 38/50 = 0.76\)
  • Posterior mean: \(\frac{44}{60} = 0.7333\) — a weighted average, pulled toward the data

Example: Gamma Prior for Exponential Data (DBC 15.2)

Proposition: Gamma-Exponential Conjugacy

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\) with pdf \(f(x|\lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\). If \(\lambda\) is assigned a Gamma(\(\alpha_0, \beta_0\)) prior, the posterior distribution of \(\lambda\) is:

\[\lambda \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha_0 + n, \;\frac{1}{1/\beta_0 + \sum x_i}\right)\]

Proof: The joint likelihood is \(f(x|\lambda) = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}\). The Gamma prior is \(\pi(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{\alpha_0 - 1} e^{-\lambda/\beta_0}\). Then:

\[\pi(\lambda|x) \propto \lambda^{\alpha_0 - 1} e^{-\lambda/\beta_0} \cdot \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i} = \lambda^{(\alpha_0 + n) - 1} \exp\!\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{1}{\beta_0} + \sum x_i\right)\right)\]

This is the kernel of a Gamma distribution with shape \(\alpha_0 + n\) and scale \(\frac{1}{1/\beta_0 + \sum x_i}\). \(\square\)

Medical Example: Gamma-Exponential — Hospital Wait Times

An emergency department models the rate \(\lambda\) (patients per hour) of arrivals. Prior experience suggests approximately 5 patients per hour, so we use a Gamma(5, 1) prior for \(\lambda\) (mean = 5).

We observe \(n = 12\) inter-arrival times (in hours) with \(\sum x_i = 2.8\).

Code 
# Gamma-Exponential: Hospital arrival rate
a0 <- 5; b0 <- 1
n_obs <- 12; sum_x <- 2.8

# Posterior
a_post_exp <- a0 + n_obs
b_post_exp <- 1 / (1/b0 + sum_x)

tibble(lambda = seq(0, 15, by = 0.01)) |>
  mutate(
    Prior = dgamma(lambda, shape = a0, scale = b0),
    Posterior = dgamma(lambda, shape = a_post_exp, scale = b_post_exp)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(-lambda, names_to = "Distribution", values_to = "density") |>
  ggplot(aes(x = lambda, y = density, color = Distribution, linetype = Distribution)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = n_obs / sum_x, linetype = "dotted", color = "gray40") +
  annotate("text", x = n_obs / sum_x + 0.3, y = 0.6,
           label = "MLE", color = "gray40", size = 5) +
  labs(
    x = expression(lambda ~ "(patients/hour)"),
    y = "Density",
    title = "Bayesian updating: ED arrival rate",
    subtitle = paste0("Prior: Gamma(", a0, ", ", b0, ") | Posterior: Gamma(",
                       a_post_exp, ", ", round(b_post_exp, 3), ")")
  ) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("Prior" = "steelblue", "Posterior" = "darkred")) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

Example: Gamma Prior for Poisson Data (C&B, C&H 10.4)

Proposition: Gamma-Poisson Conjugacy

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\theta)\). If \(\theta\) is assigned a Gamma(\(\alpha_0, \beta_0\)) prior, then:

\[\theta \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha_0 + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \; \frac{1}{n + 1/\beta_0}\right)\]

Proof: The joint pmf is \(f(x|\theta) = \frac{\theta^{\sum x_i} e^{-n\theta}}{\prod x_i!}\). Combining with the Gamma prior \(\pi(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha_0 - 1} e^{-\theta/\beta_0}\):

\[\pi(\theta|x) \propto \theta^{\alpha_0 - 1} e^{-\theta/\beta_0} \cdot \theta^{\sum x_i} e^{-n\theta} = \theta^{(\alpha_0 + \sum x_i) - 1} \exp\!\left(-\theta\left(n + \frac{1}{\beta_0}\right)\right)\]

which is the Gamma\(\!\left(\alpha_0 + \sum x_i, \; \frac{1}{n + 1/\beta_0}\right)\) kernel. \(\square\)

Example: Normal Prior for Normal Mean (DBC 15.9, C&H 10.4)

Proposition: Normal-Normal Conjugacy

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2\) known. If \(\mu\) is assigned a \(N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) prior, then:

\[\mu \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n \sim N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\]

where the posterior precision (reciprocal of variance) is:

\[\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}\]

and the posterior mean is a weighted average:

\[\mu_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}}{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}} \cdot \mu_0 + \frac{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}} \cdot \bar{x}\]

Key insight — Precision adds:

\[\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}}_{\text{posterior precision}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}}_{\text{prior precision}} + \underbrace{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}_{\text{data precision}}\]

The weights in the posterior mean are proportional to the respective precisions!

Medical Example: Normal-Normal — Systolic Blood Pressure

A researcher studies the mean systolic blood pressure \(\mu\) in a hypertensive population. Prior clinical knowledge suggests \(\mu \approx 145\) mmHg with \(\sigma_0 = 10\). Population \(\sigma = 20\) mmHg is assumed known.

A sample of \(n = 25\) patients has \(\bar{x} = 152\) mmHg.

Code 
# Normal-Normal: Blood pressure
mu0 <- 145; sigma0 <- 10    # Prior
sigma <- 20                  # Known population SD
n_bp <- 25; xbar <- 152     # Data

# Posterior parameters
sigma1_sq <- 1 / (1/sigma0^2 + n_bp/sigma^2)
sigma1 <- sqrt(sigma1_sq)
mu1 <- sigma1_sq * (mu0/sigma0^2 + n_bp * xbar / sigma^2)

tibble(mu = seq(120, 175, by = 0.1)) |>
  mutate(
    Prior = dnorm(mu, mu0, sigma0),
    `Data likelihood` = dnorm(mu, xbar, sigma / sqrt(n_bp)),
    Posterior = dnorm(mu, mu1, sigma1)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(-mu, names_to = "Distribution", values_to = "density") |>
  mutate(Distribution = factor(Distribution, levels = c("Prior", "Data likelihood", "Posterior"))) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = mu, y = density, color = Distribution, linetype = Distribution)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  labs(
    x = expression(mu ~ "(mmHg)"),
    y = "Density",
    title = "Normal-Normal conjugate update: Systolic blood pressure",
    subtitle = bquote("Prior:" ~ mu[0] == .(mu0) * "," ~ sigma[0] == .(sigma0) ~
                       "| Data:" ~ bar(x) == .(xbar) * "," ~ n == .(n_bp) ~
                       "| Posterior:" ~ mu[1] == .(round(mu1, 1)) * "," ~ sigma[1] == .(round(sigma1, 2)))
  ) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("Prior" = "steelblue", "Data likelihood" = "gray50", "Posterior" = "darkred")) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")
  • Prior mean: \(\mu_0 = 145\), Data mean: \(\bar{x} = 152\)
  • Posterior mean: \(\mu_1 = 151.03\) — between the two, weighted by precision
  • Posterior SD: \(\sigma_1 = 3.71\) — always smaller than both \(\sigma_0\) and \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\)

Proof: Normal-Normal Conjugacy

Proof sketch: Apply the posterior formula with \(\pi(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_0} e^{-(\mu - \mu_0)^2/(2\sigma_0^2)}\) and the normal likelihood:

\[\pi(\mu|x) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\right) \prod_{i=1}^n \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

Expanding the exponent and collecting terms in \(\mu\):

\[\pi(\mu|x) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{\sum(x_i - \mu)^2}{\sigma^2}\right]\right)\]

Completing the square in \(\mu\) (the key algebraic step):

\[= \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\mu - \mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2} + C\right)\]

where \(C\) does not depend on \(\mu\), and \(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2\) are as defined in the proposition.

Since this is proportional to a \(N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\) density in \(\mu\), the posterior must be \(N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\). \(\square\)

Bayesian Point Estimation

The Bayes Estimator

Definition: Bayes Estimator (DBC 15.2)

The most common Bayesian point estimator of \(\theta\) is the mean of the posterior distribution:

\[\hat{\theta}_B = E(\theta \mid X_1, \ldots, X_n)\]

An alternative is the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate — the mode of the posterior distribution.

For our conjugate families:

Model Bayes Estimator (Posterior Mean)
Beta(\(\alpha_0 + y, \beta_0 + n - y\)) \(\hat{p}_B = \frac{\alpha_0 + y}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n}\)
Gamma(\(\alpha_0 + n, \frac{1}{1/\beta_0 + \sum x_i}\)) \(\hat{\lambda}_B = \frac{\alpha_0 + n}{1/\beta_0 + \sum x_i}\)
\(N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\) \(\hat{\mu}_B = \mu_1\) (weighted average of \(\mu_0\) and \(\bar{x}\))

The Bayes Estimator as a Weighted Average

For the Binomial-Beta case, the Bayes estimator can be written as:

\[\hat{p}_B = \frac{\alpha_0 + y}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n} = \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_0 + \beta_0}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n}}_{\text{weight on prior}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0}}_{\text{prior mean}} + \underbrace{\frac{n}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n}}_{\text{weight on data}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{y}{n}}_{\text{sample proportion}}\]

As \(n \to \infty\): the weight on the prior \(\to 0\) and the weight on the data \(\to 1\).

\[\hat{p}_B \to \frac{y}{n} = \hat{p}_{MLE}\]

The data dominates the prior for large samples.

Properties of Bayes Estimators

Under general conditions, as \(n \to \infty\):

  1. The mean of the posterior distribution converges to the true value of \(\theta\)
  2. The variance of the posterior distribution converges to zero

These properties imply that Bayes estimators are generally consistent (in the frequentist sense).

Key consequence: For large samples, Bayesian and frequentist estimates will typically be quite close, regardless of the prior chosen (as long as the prior is not degenerate — i.e., assigns zero probability to the true value).

For small samples, the prior can have a substantial influence. This is a feature (incorporating prior knowledge) or a bug (sensitivity to subjective choices), depending on one’s perspective.

Simulation: Effect of Sample Size on Prior Influence

Code 
# Show how the posterior evolves with increasing n
# True p = 0.7, Prior: Beta(3, 7) centered at 0.3
a0 <- 3; b0 <- 7  # Prior mean = 0.3
true_p <- 0.7

set.seed(551)
sample_sizes <- c(0, 5, 20, 50, 200)
plot_data <- map_dfr(sample_sizes, function(n_i) {
  if (n_i == 0) {
    y_i <- 0
  } else {
    y_i <- rbinom(1, n_i, true_p)
  }
  tibble(p = seq(0, 1, by = 0.002)) |>
    mutate(
      density = dbeta(p, a0 + y_i, b0 + n_i - y_i),
      n = paste0("n = ", n_i, ifelse(n_i > 0, paste0(" (y = ", y_i, ")"), " (prior)"))
    )
})

plot_data |>
  mutate(n = fct_inorder(n)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = density, color = n)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = true_p, linetype = "dashed", color = "gray40") +
  annotate("text", x = true_p + 0.03, y = 0.5,
           label = paste0("True p = ", true_p), color = "gray40", hjust = 0, size = 5) +
  labs(
    x = expression(p), y = "Posterior density",
    title = "Posterior distribution evolves toward the truth as n increases",
    subtitle = "Prior: Beta(3, 7), centered at 0.3 — far from the true p = 0.7"
  ) +
  scale_color_viridis_d(name = "Sample size", option = "C", end = 0.85) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

Bayesian Hypothesis Testing

Bayesian Approach to Hypothesis Testing

In frequentist testing, \(\theta\) is fixed and a hypothesis is either true or false. The probabilities \(P(H_0 \text{ is true} | x)\) and \(P(H_1 \text{ is true} | x)\) are simply 0 or 1.

In Bayesian testing, \(\theta\) has a distribution, so we can directly compute:

\[P(H_0 \text{ is true} | x) = P(\theta \in \Theta_0 | x) = \int_{\Theta_0} \pi(\theta|x) \, d\theta\]

\[P(H_1 \text{ is true} | x) = P(\theta \in \Theta_0^c | x) = \int_{\Theta_0^c} \pi(\theta|x) \, d\theta\]

Decision rules (examples):

  • Accept \(H_0\) if \(P(\theta \in \Theta_0 | x) \geq P(\theta \in \Theta_0^c | x)\)
  • Accept \(H_0\) if \(P(\theta \in \Theta_0 | x) > 0.95\) (or some high threshold)

Medical Example: Bayesian Test — Drug Response Rate

A new cancer drug is tested. We want to assess:

\[H_0: p \leq 0.30 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: p > 0.30\]

where \(p\) is the response rate. We use a Beta(2, 5) prior (prior mean \(\approx 0.29\), reflecting skepticism).

In the trial, \(n = 40\) patients are treated and \(y = 18\) respond.

Code 
# Bayesian hypothesis test
a0_drug <- 2; b0_drug <- 5
n_drug <- 40; y_drug <- 18

a_post_drug <- a0_drug + y_drug    # 20
b_post_drug <- b0_drug + n_drug - y_drug  # 27

# Posterior probability of H0
p_H0 <- pbeta(0.30, a_post_drug, b_post_drug)
p_H1 <- 1 - p_H0

tibble(p = seq(0, 1, by = 0.001)) |>
  mutate(posterior = dbeta(p, a_post_drug, b_post_drug)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = posterior)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  geom_area(data = . %>% filter(p <= 0.30),
            aes(x = p, y = posterior), fill = "steelblue", alpha = 0.3) +
  geom_area(data = . %>% filter(p > 0.30),
            aes(x = p, y = posterior), fill = "darkred", alpha = 0.3) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0.30, linetype = "dashed") +
  annotate("text", x = 0.20, y = 2.5,
           label = paste0("P(H0) = ", round(p_H0, 3)),
           color = "steelblue", size = 6) +
  annotate("text", x = 0.55, y = 2.5,
           label = paste0("P(H1) = ", round(p_H1, 3)),
           color = "darkred", size = 6) +
  labs(
    x = expression(p), y = "Posterior density",
    title = "Bayesian hypothesis test: Drug response rate",
    subtitle = paste0("Posterior: Beta(", a_post_drug, ", ", b_post_drug,
                       ") | P(p > 0.30 | data) = ", round(p_H1, 3))
  )

The posterior probability that \(p > 0.30\) is \(0.964\), providing strong evidence that the drug’s response rate exceeds 30%.

Example: Bayesian Test with Bernoulli Data (C&B 8.2.2)

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim}\) Bernoulli(\(p\)) with prior \(p \sim\) Uniform(0, 1) = Beta(1, 1).

\[H_0: p \leq 1/2 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: p > 1/2\]

The posterior is Beta\((\sum x_i + 1, \; n - \sum x_i + 1)\).

Decision rule: Reject \(H_0\) if \(P(p \leq 1/2 \mid x) \leq 0.5\).

Case 1: \(n = 20\), \(\sum x_i = 11\). Then \(p|x \sim\) Beta(12, 10) and:

\[P(p \leq 0.5 \mid x) = 0.332\]

Since this is less than 0.5, we would reject \(H_0\) and conclude \(p > 1/2\).

Case 2: \(n = 20\), \(\sum x_i = 9\). Then \(p|x \sim\) Beta(10, 12) and:

\[P(p \leq 0.5 \mid x) = 0.668\]

Since this exceeds 0.5, we would not reject \(H_0\).

Bayesian Interval Estimation

Credible Intervals

Definition: Credible Interval

For a posterior distribution \(\pi(\theta|x)\), an interval \([a, b]\) having posterior probability \(1 - \alpha\) is called a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) credible interval:

\[P(a \leq \theta \leq b \mid x) = \int_a^b \pi(\theta|x) \, d\theta = 1 - \alpha\]

The standard approach is an equal-tailed credible interval: choose \(a\) and \(b\) as the \(\alpha/2\) and \(1 - \alpha/2\) quantiles of the posterior distribution.

An alternative is the highest posterior density (HPD) credible interval: the shortest interval with the required posterior probability. For symmetric, unimodal posteriors, the equal-tailed and HPD intervals coincide.

Credible Intervals vs. Confidence Intervals

Frequentist Confidence Interval:

  • The parameter \(\theta\) is fixed (not random)
  • The interval \([L(X), U(X)]\) is random
  • Interpretation: If we repeated the experiment many times and computed an interval each time, \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) of those intervals would contain the true \(\theta\)
  • We do not say “\(P(\theta \in [L, U]) = 0.95\)”

Bayesian Credible Interval:

  • The parameter \(\theta\) is random
  • The interval \([a, b]\) is fixed (given observed data)
  • Interpretation: There is a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) probability that \(\theta\) lies in \([a, b]\)
  • We can say “\(P(\theta \in [a, b] \mid x) = 0.95\)”

For large samples, credible intervals and confidence intervals are often numerically similar, but their interpretations remain fundamentally different.

Example: Credible Intervals for Vaccine Efficacy

Continuing our vaccine example (Beta(44, 16) posterior):

Code 
a_post_v <- 44; b_post_v <- 16
alpha <- 0.05

# Equal-tailed credible interval
ci_lower <- qbeta(alpha/2, a_post_v, b_post_v)
ci_upper <- qbeta(1 - alpha/2, a_post_v, b_post_v)

# Frequentist Wald CI for comparison
p_hat <- 38/50
freq_lower <- p_hat - qnorm(1 - alpha/2) * sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / 50)
freq_upper <- p_hat + qnorm(1 - alpha/2) * sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / 50)

tibble(p = seq(0.4, 1, by = 0.001)) |>
  mutate(posterior = dbeta(p, a_post_v, b_post_v)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = posterior)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  geom_area(data = . %>% filter(p >= ci_lower & p <= ci_upper),
            fill = "darkred", alpha = 0.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(ci_lower, ci_upper), color = "darkred", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_segment(aes(x = freq_lower, xend = freq_upper, y = -0.2, yend = -0.2),
               color = "steelblue", linewidth = 2) +
  annotate("text", x = mean(c(freq_lower, freq_upper)), y = -0.5,
           label = "Frequentist Wald CI", color = "steelblue", size = 4.5) +

Example: Poisson Credible Interval (C&B 9.2.16)

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim}\) Poisson(\(\theta\)) with sufficient statistic \(T = \sum X_i\) and prior Gamma(\(\alpha, \beta\)).

The posterior is Gamma\((\alpha + t, \; 1/(n + 1/\beta))\).

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) credible interval splits \(\alpha\) equally between the tails of this posterior.

Using the Gamma–chi-squared relationship, if \(\alpha\) is an integer, the credible interval can be expressed using chi-squared quantiles:

\[\left[\frac{\beta}{2(\beta n + 1)} \chi^2_{2(\alpha + t), 1-\alpha/2}, \quad \frac{\beta}{2(\beta n + 1)} \chi^2_{2(\alpha + t), \alpha/2}\right]\]

Compare to the frequentist confidence interval from pivoting the CDF (Lesson 8):

\[\left[\frac{1}{2n} \chi^2_{2t, 1-\alpha/2}, \quad \frac{1}{2n} \chi^2_{2(t+1), \alpha/2}\right]\]

The Bayesian interval incorporates the prior through \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\); the frequentist interval depends only on the data.

Poisson Credible Interval: Soccer Goals Example

Data from the English Premier League: \(n = 380\) games, total goals \(t = 1060\).

Code 
# Poisson credible interval: Soccer example from class notes
nn <- 380
tstat <- 1060

# Prior: Gamma(1.5, 2)
aa <- 1.5; bb <- 2
astar <- tstat + aa
bstar <- 1 / (nn + 1/bb)

alpha_ci <- 0.05
low_bayes <- qgamma(alpha_ci/2, shape = astar, scale = bstar)
upper_bayes <- qgamma(1 - alpha_ci/2, shape = astar, scale = bstar)

# Frequentist CI using chi-squared pivot
low_freq <- qchisq(p = 1 - alpha_ci/2, df = 2 * tstat, lower.tail = FALSE) / (2 * nn)
upper_freq <- qchisq(p = alpha_ci/2, df = 2 * (tstat + 1), lower.tail = FALSE) / (2 * nn)

tibble(theta = seq(2.4, 3.2, by = 0.001)) |>
  mutate(posterior = dgamma(theta, shape = astar, scale = bstar)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = theta, y = posterior)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(low_bayes, upper_bayes), color = "darkred", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(low_freq, upper_freq), color = "steelblue", linetype = "dotted") +
  annotate("text", x = 2.5, y = max(dgamma(seq(2.4, 3.2, 0.01), astar, scale = bstar)) * 0.9,
           label = paste0("Bayes: [", round(low_bayes, 3), ", ", round(upper_bayes, 3), "]"),
           color = "darkred", size = 5, hjust = 0) +
  annotate("text", x = 2.5, y = max(dgamma(seq(2.4, 3.2, 0.01), astar, scale = bstar)) * 0.75,
           label = paste0("Freq: [", round(low_freq, 3), ", ", round(upper_freq, 3), "]"),
           color = "steelblue", size = 5, hjust = 0) +
  labs(
    x = expression(theta ~ "(goals per game)"),
    y = "Posterior density",
    title = "Poisson credible interval: Mean goals per game (EPL)",
    subtitle = paste0("n = ", nn, " games, total goals = ", tstat,
                       ", Prior: Gamma(1.5, 2), Posterior: Gamma(", astar, ", ", round(bstar, 5), ")")
  )

With \(n = 380\), the Bayesian and frequentist intervals are nearly identical — the data overwhelms the prior.

Noninformative Priors and Sensitivity

Noninformative (Flat) Priors

When little prior knowledge is available, we may want a prior that has minimal influence on the posterior. Common choices include:

  • Beta(1, 1) = Uniform(0,1) for a proportion — the “flat” prior
  • Beta(0.5, 0.5) — Jeffreys’ prior for a binomial proportion
  • \(\pi(\mu) \propto 1\) for a normal mean — an improper prior (does not integrate to a finite value, but yields a proper posterior)

With the flat prior Beta(1, 1) for binomial data, the posterior is Beta(\(1 + y, 1 + n - y\)), and:

\[\hat{p}_B = \frac{1 + y}{2 + n}\]

This is the Laplace estimator — equivalent to adding one prior success and one prior failure.

Noninformative priors let the data “speak for themselves” and produce results similar to frequentist methods. The posterior mean with a flat prior is approximately the MLE for large \(n\).

Prior Sensitivity Analysis

A responsible Bayesian analysis should assess how sensitive the conclusions are to the choice of prior. This is called sensitivity analysis or robustness analysis.

Code 
# Sensitivity to prior choice
n_sens <- 20; y_sens <- 14

priors <- tribble(
  ~name, ~a0, ~b0,
  "Flat: Beta(1, 1)", 1, 1,
  "Skeptical: Beta(2, 8)", 2, 8,
  "Optimistic: Beta(8, 2)", 8, 2,
  "Jeffreys: Beta(0.5, 0.5)", 0.5, 0.5
)

plot_data_sens <- priors |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(data = list(
    tibble(p = seq(0, 1, by = 0.002)) |>
      mutate(
        prior = dbeta(p, a0, b0),
        posterior = dbeta(p, a0 + y_sens, b0 + n_sens - y_sens)
      )
  )) |>
  unnest(data) |>
  ungroup()

p_prior <- plot_data_sens |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = prior, color = name)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  labs(x = expression(p), y = "Density", title = "Prior distributions") +
  scale_color_brewer(palette = "Set1", name = "Prior") +
  theme(legend.position = "none")

p_post <- plot_data_sens |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = posterior, color = name)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = y_sens / n_sens, linetype = "dotted", color = "gray40") +
  labs(x = expression(p), y = "Density",
       title = paste0("Posterior distributions (n = ", n_sens, ", y = ", y_sens, ")")) +
  scale_color_brewer(palette = "Set1", name = "Prior") +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

p_prior + p_post + plot_layout(widths = c(1, 1.2))

Even with \(n = 20\), the posteriors are already converging. With larger \(n\), the choice of prior matters even less.

Bayesian vs. Frequentist: Summary

Side-by-Side Comparison

Aspect Frequentist Bayesian
Parameter \(\theta\) Fixed, unknown constant Random variable with a distribution
Probability Long-run frequency Degree of belief
Prior information Not formally incorporated Encoded in \(\pi(\theta)\)
Point estimate MLE \(\hat{\theta}\) Posterior mean \(E(\theta|x)\)
Interval estimate Confidence interval Credible interval
Hypothesis test P-value, rejection region Posterior probability \(P(H_0|x)\)
Interpretation of 95% CI 95% of intervals from repeated samples cover \(\theta\) 95% probability \(\theta\) is in the interval
Large sample behavior Asymptotic normality via CLT Posterior concentrates on true \(\theta\)
Agreement For large \(n\), methods typically agree

As C&B state: “There is no one best way to do statistics; some problems are best solved with classical statistics and some are best solved with Bayesian statistics.”

Summary and Connections

Key Takeaways

  1. Bayes’ theorem provides a principled way to update beliefs: \(\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Prior} \times \text{Likelihood}\)

  2. Conjugate priors yield tractable posteriors in the same family as the prior

  3. The Bayes estimator (posterior mean) is a weighted average of prior belief and observed data, with weights determined by relative precision

  4. Credible intervals allow direct probability statements about parameters

  5. For large samples, Bayesian and frequentist methods converge; differences matter most for small samples or strong priors

  6. Bayesian methods offer a coherent framework for combining prior knowledge with data — particularly valuable in medical research where historical data and expert knowledge are abundant

Connection to Course Themes

Throughout BSTA 551, we have studied the foundations of statistical inference:

  • Point estimation: MLE, method of moments, MVUE, sufficiency, Fisher information (Lessons 1–6)
  • Interval estimation: Confidence intervals, bootstrap CIs (Lessons 7–11)
  • Hypothesis testing: P-values, power, Neyman-Pearson, LRT (Lessons 12–17)
  • Two-sample and nonparametric methods (Lessons 18–22)

Bayesian inference provides an alternative paradigm that:

  • Uses the same likelihood function as frequentist methods
  • Reaches similar conclusions for large samples (consistency of Bayes estimators)
  • Offers different — sometimes more intuitive — interpretations of uncertainty
  • Formalizes the incorporation of prior knowledge into analysis
