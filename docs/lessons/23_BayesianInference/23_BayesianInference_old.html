Lesson 23: Introduction to Bayesian Inference

Review: The Frequentist Framework

Everything we have built this quarter rests on a single foundation:

  • Parameters (\(\theta\)) are fixed, unknown constants
  • Data (\(X_1, \ldots, X_n\)) are random
  • Inference is based on the sampling distribution of estimators and test statistics

. . .

What we have accomplished:

Framework Tool Key Idea
Point estimation MLE, MOM, MVUE \(\hat\theta\) chosen to summarize the data
Interval estimation CI, bootstrap CI Procedure covers \(\theta\) with prob \(1-\alpha\)
Hypothesis testing NP, LRT, permutation Control Type I error; reject \(H_0\) if evidence extreme

. . .

NoteA Different Question

What if we treated \(\theta\) as random — capturing our uncertainty about its value — and updated our beliefs when we observe data?

This is the Bayesian approach, and it underpins a large portion of modern biostatistical and machine learning methodology.

Today’s Goals

  1. Understand the philosophical distinction between Bayesian and frequentist inference
  2. Formulate Bayes’ theorem for continuous parameters: prior → posterior
  3. Identify conjugate prior families for common likelihoods (Beta–Binomial, Normal–Normal, Gamma–Poisson)
  4. Compute credible intervals and contrast them with confidence intervals
  5. Derive Bayes estimators as minimizers of posterior expected loss under squared and absolute error
  6. See these ideas applied in medical research contexts

The Bayesian Philosophy

Parameters as Random Variables

In the frequentist framework, \(\theta\) is a fixed constant. We ask:

“What would happen if we repeated this experiment many times?”

. . .

In the Bayesian framework, \(\theta\) is treated as a random variable with its own distribution. We ask:

“Given the data I actually observed, what do I now believe about \(\theta\)?”

. . .

ImportantThe Bayesian Ingredients

Three quantities determine everything:

  1. Prior distribution \(\pi(\theta)\): encodes beliefs about \(\theta\) before observing data
  2. Likelihood \(L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta)\): the probability of the data given \(\theta\)
  3. Posterior distribution \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})\): updated beliefs about \(\theta\) after observing data

. . .

All Bayesian inference flows from the posterior distribution.

Bayes’ Theorem for Parameters

For a continuous parameter \(\theta\) and observed data \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\):

\[\boxed{\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)}{f(\mathbf{x})} = \frac{f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)}{\int f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta') \, \pi(\theta') \, d\theta'}}\]

. . .

Since the marginal likelihood \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \int f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta') \pi(\theta') d\theta'\) does not depend on \(\theta\), we often write:

\[\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta) = L(\theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)\]

. . .

In words:

\[\underbrace{\text{Posterior}}_{\text{updated beliefs}} \propto \underbrace{\text{Likelihood}}_{\text{evidence from data}} \times \underbrace{\text{Prior}}_{\text{initial beliefs}}\]

Medical Motivation: Estimating Disease Prevalence

TipRunning Example

A public health researcher wants to estimate \(p\), the true prevalence of a rare genetic condition in a population. A clinical study observes \(X\) cases among \(n\) patients.

  • Frequentist: \(\hat{p} = X/n\) with CI \(\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\)
  • Bayesian: Start with a prior \(\pi(p)\) based on historical knowledge, observe data, and compute \(\pi(p \mid X)\)

. . .

Why might Bayesian methods be attractive here?

  • If the condition is rare (\(n\) is small, \(X\) may be 0 or 1), the MLE is unstable
  • We often have historical data or domain expertise that should inform the estimate
  • Regulatory agencies (e.g., FDA) increasingly accept Bayesian designs for clinical trials
  • Bayesian estimates naturally shrink toward prior beliefs, reducing variance

Prior Distributions

Choosing a Prior

The prior \(\pi(\theta)\) encodes our uncertainty about \(\theta\) before data are collected. Three common choices:

. . .

Informative priors: reflect genuine prior knowledge \[\text{e.g., } p \sim \text{Beta}(3, 17) \text{ if historical data suggest prevalence} \approx 15\%\]

. . .

Weakly informative priors: constrain parameters to plausible ranges without dominating the data \[\text{e.g., } p \sim \text{Beta}(1.5, 1.5) \text{ — slightly favors interior values}\]

. . .

Non-informative (flat) priors: minimal prior assumptions; let data dominate

\[\text{e.g., } p \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1) = \text{Uniform}(0,1) \text{ — all values of } p \text{ equally likely}\]

. . .

NoteJeffreys Prior

A principled non-informative prior is the Jeffreys prior: \(\pi(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}\), where \(I(\theta)\) is the Fisher information. For a Binomial proportion, the Jeffreys prior is \(\text{Beta}(1/2, 1/2)\).

Conjugate Prior Families

What is a Conjugate Prior?

ImportantDefinition: Conjugate Prior

A prior \(\pi(\theta)\) is conjugate to the likelihood \(f(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta)\) if the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})\) belongs to the same parametric family as the prior.

. . .

Conjugate priors are computationally convenient:

  • Posterior has a closed-form expression
  • Posterior parameters have intuitive data-update interpretations
  • Sequential Bayesian updating is trivial: the posterior after \(n\) observations becomes the prior for observation \(n+1\)

. . .

We will study three conjugate families:

Likelihood Conjugate Prior Posterior
Binomial\((n, p)\) Beta\((\alpha, \beta)\) Beta\((\alpha + x,\; \beta + n - x)\)
Normal\((\mu, \sigma^2)\), \(\sigma^2\) known Normal\((\mu_0, \tau_0^2)\) Normal\((\mu_n, \tau_n^2)\)
Poisson\((\lambda)\) Gamma\((\alpha, \beta)\) Gamma\((\alpha + \sum x_i,\; \beta + n)\)

Beta–Binomial Conjugate Family

Setup: \(X \mid p \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)\), prior \(p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\)

. . .

Posterior derivation:

\[\pi(p \mid x) \propto L(p) \cdot \pi(p) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} \cdot \frac{p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\]

\[\propto p^{x + \alpha - 1}(1-p)^{n-x+\beta-1}\]

. . .

This is the kernel of a \(\text{Beta}(x + \alpha,\; n - x + \beta)\) distribution:

\[\boxed{p \mid x \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + x, \; \beta + n - x)}\]

. . .

TipIntuition

The prior parameters \((\alpha, \beta)\) act like pseudo-counts: \(\alpha - 1\) prior successes and \(\beta - 1\) prior failures. Observing \(x\) successes and \(n-x\) failures simply adds to these counts.

Beta–Binomial: Prior-to-Posterior Updating

Example: Estimating prevalence of a rare genetic condition.

  • Historical data suggests prevalence \(\approx 15\%\), with uncertainty \(\Rightarrow\) Prior: \(p \sim \text{Beta}(3, 17)\)
  • New study: \(n = 50\) patients, \(x = 10\) test positive
Code 
# Prior parameters
alpha0 <- 3; beta0 <- 17

# Data
n_obs <- 50; x_obs <- 10

# Posterior parameters
alpha1 <- alpha0 + x_obs
beta1  <- beta0 + n_obs - x_obs

# MLE
mle <- x_obs / n_obs

tibble(p = seq(0.001, 0.999, length.out = 600)) |>
  mutate(
    Prior     = dbeta(p, alpha0, beta0),
    Posterior = dbeta(p, alpha1, beta1),
    Likelihood = dbeta(p, x_obs + 1, n_obs - x_obs + 1) # proportional to likelihood
  ) |>
  # Normalize likelihood for display
  mutate(Likelihood = Likelihood / max(Likelihood) * max(Posterior)) |>
  pivot_longer(-p, names_to = "Distribution", values_to = "density") |>
  mutate(Distribution = factor(Distribution, levels = c("Prior", "Likelihood", "Posterior"))) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = density, color = Distribution, linetype = Distribution)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mle, linetype = "dashed", color = "gray40") +
  annotate("text", x = mle + 0.01, y = 0.5, label = "MLE", hjust = 0, size = 5, color = "gray40") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = alpha1 / (alpha1 + beta1), linetype = "dotted", color = "#e74c3c") +
  annotate("text", x = alpha1/(alpha1+beta1) + 0.01, y = 2,

  scale_color_manual(values = c("Prior" = "#3498db", "Likelihood" = "#2ecc71", "Posterior" = "#e74c3c")) +

Normal–Normal Conjugate Family

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \mid \mu \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), \(\sigma^2\) known

Prior: \(\mu \sim N(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)\)

. . .

Posterior derivation (completing the square in the exponent):

\[\pi(\mu \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{n(\bar{x} - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \cdot \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}\right)\]

\[\propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\mu - \mu_n)^2}{2\tau_n^2}\right)\]

. . .

where the posterior parameters are:

\[\boxed{\frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu_n = \frac{\mu_0/\tau_0^2 + n\bar{x}/\sigma^2}{1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2}}\]

\[\mu \mid \mathbf{x} \sim N(\mu_n, \, \tau_n^2)\]

TipPrecision Weighting

Define precision = \(1/\text{variance}\). The posterior precision is the sum of prior and data precisions. The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of \(\mu_0\) and \(\bar{x}\).

Normal–Normal: Blood Pressure Example

TipMedical Example

A cardiologist wants to estimate mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) for a new patient population.

  • Historical data from similar populations: mean SBP \(\approx 128\) mmHg, \(\sigma_{\text{prior}} = 10\) mmHg
  • Known measurement \(\sigma = 15\) mmHg
  • New study: \(n = 30\), \(\bar{x} = 135\) mmHg
Code 
mu0   <- 128; tau0  <- 10
xbar  <- 135; sigma <- 15; n_obs <- 30

# Posterior parameters
prec0 <- 1/tau0^2
prec_data <- n_obs/sigma^2
prec_n    <- prec0 + prec_data
tau_n     <- sqrt(1/prec_n)
mu_n      <- (mu0 * prec0 + xbar * prec_data) / prec_n

tibble(mu = seq(100, 160, length.out = 600)) |>
  mutate(
    Prior     = dnorm(mu, mu0, tau0),
    Likelihood = dnorm(mu, xbar, sigma/sqrt(n_obs)),
    Posterior = dnorm(mu, mu_n, tau_n)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(-mu, names_to = "Distribution", values_to = "density") |>
  mutate(Distribution = factor(Distribution, levels = c("Prior", "Likelihood", "Posterior"))) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = mu, y = density, color = Distribution, linetype = Distribution)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mu_n, linetype = "solid", color = "#e74c3c", alpha = 0.4) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = mu0,  linetype = "dashed", color = "#3498db", alpha = 0.4) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = xbar, linetype = "dotted", color = "#2ecc71", alpha = 0.4) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("Prior" = "#3498db", "Likelihood" = "#2ecc71", "Posterior" = "#e74c3c")) +
  scale_linetype_manual(values = c("Prior" = "dashed", "Likelihood" = "dotted", "Posterior" = "solid")) +

Gamma–Poisson Conjugate Family

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \mid \lambda \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\)

Prior: \(\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) with mean \(\alpha/\beta\)

. . .

Posterior derivation:

\[\pi(\lambda \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!} \cdot \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda}\]

\[\propto \lambda^{\alpha + \sum x_i - 1} e^{-(\beta + n)\lambda}\]

. . .

\[\boxed{\lambda \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \;\; \beta + n\right)}\]

. . .

TipMedical Example

Adverse event monitoring: A clinical trial monitors rare adverse events. Prior knowledge suggests \(\lambda \approx 0.5\) events/patient-month. Data from \(n = 40\) patients: \(\sum x_i = 25\) events.

\[\text{Prior: Gamma}(2, 4) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{Posterior: Gamma}(2 + 25, 4 + 40) = \text{Gamma}(27, 44)\]

Posterior mean \(= 27/44 \approx 0.614\) events/patient-month

Credible Intervals

What is a Credible Interval?

ImportantDefinition: Credible Interval

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) credible interval (also called a Bayesian interval or posterior interval) is a set \(C(\mathbf{x})\) such that

\[P(\theta \in C(\mathbf{x}) \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1 - \alpha\]

The probability is with respect to the posterior distribution \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})\).

. . .

This is the interval that students think a frequentist CI gives!

. . .

Two common choices:

1. Equal-tail credible interval: \(\left[Q_{\alpha/2},\; Q_{1-\alpha/2}\right]\) where \(Q_p\) is the \(p\)-th quantile of the posterior

2. Highest posterior density (HPD) interval: the shortest interval containing \(1-\alpha\) posterior probability

\[C_{\text{HPD}} = \{\theta : \pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \geq k_\alpha\}\]

where \(k_\alpha\) is chosen so that \(P(\theta \in C_{\text{HPD}} \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1-\alpha\).

Comparing Credible Intervals and Confidence Intervals

Property Frequentist CI Bayesian Credible Interval
What is random? The interval (over repeated samples) The parameter \(\theta\)
Correct interpretation “95% of such intervals contain \(\theta\)” “95% probability that \(\theta\) is in this interval”
Depends on prior? No Yes
Valid for small \(n\)? Approximately Yes (via posterior)
Can be asymmetric? Typically symmetric Naturally asymmetric for skewed posteriors

. . .

NoteThe Interpretation Students Want

A 95% credible interval literally means: given the observed data, there is a 95% probability that \(\theta\) lies in the interval. This is the statement frequentist CIs cannot make.

Computing Credible Intervals: Beta–Binomial

Code 
alpha1 <- 13; beta1 <- 53

# Equal-tail CI
et_lo <- qbeta(0.025, alpha1, beta1)
et_hi <- qbeta(0.975, alpha1, beta1)

# HPD: search for shortest interval
# Use grid search for HPD
find_hpd <- function(a, b, level = 0.95) {
  grid <- seq(0.0001, 1 - level - 0.0001, length.out = 2000)
  widths <- qbeta(grid + level, a, b) - qbeta(grid, a, b)
  lo_prob <- grid[which.min(widths)]
  c(qbeta(lo_prob, a, b), qbeta(lo_prob + level, a, b))
}
hpd <- find_hpd(alpha1, beta1)

tibble(p = seq(0.001, 0.65, length.out = 600)) |>
  mutate(density = dbeta(p, alpha1, beta1)) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = p, y = density)) +
  # HPD region
  geom_area(
    data = ~ filter(.x, p >= hpd[1] & p <= hpd[2]),
    aes(fill = "HPD 95%"), alpha = 0.3
  ) +
  # Equal-tail region
  geom_ribbon(
    data = ~ filter(.x, p >= et_lo & p <= et_hi),
    aes(ymin = 0, ymax = density, fill = "Equal-tail 95%"), alpha = 0.2
  ) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.2, color = "#e74c3c") +
  geom_segment(aes(x = et_lo, xend = et_hi, y = -0.15, yend = -0.15),
               color = "#3498db", linewidth = 2, arrow = arrow(ends = "both", length = unit(0.1, "inches"))) +
  annotate("text", x = (et_lo + et_hi)/2, y = -0.6, 
           label = "Equal-tail 95% CI", hjust = 0.5, size = 4.5, color = "#3498db") +
  geom_segment(aes(x = hpd[1], xend = hpd[2], y = -1.0, yend = -1.0),
               color = "#2ecc71", linewidth = 2, arrow = arrow(ends = "both", length = unit(0.1, "inches"))) +
  annotate("text", x = (hpd[1] + hpd[2])/2, y = -1.45, 
           label = "HPD 95% CI", hjust = 0.5, size = 4.5, color = "#2ecc71") +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("HPD 95%" = "#2ecc71", "Equal-tail 95%" = "#3498db")) +
  labs(
    x = expression(italic(p)),
    y = "Posterior density",
    title = expression("95% credible intervals: Beta("*alpha[1]*","*beta[1]*") posterior"),
    subtitle = bquote("Posterior: Beta("*.(alpha1)*", "*.(beta1)*
                       ")   ET: ["*.(round(et_lo,3))*", "*.(round(et_hi,3))*
                       "]   HPD: ["*.(round(hpd[1],3))*", "*.(round(hpd[2],3))*"]"),
    fill = NULL
  ) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom") +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(-2, NA))

Sequential Bayesian Updating

One of the most powerful features of the Bayesian framework: today’s posterior becomes tomorrow’s prior.

Code 
# Start with Uniform prior; observe data sequentially
alpha0 <- 1; beta0 <- 1
# Simulated sequence of patients (true p = 0.15)
set.seed(551)
results <- rbinom(60, 1, prob = 0.15)  # 0 = negative, 1 = positive

check_points <- c(0, 5, 15, 30, 60)

plots_data <- map_dfr(check_points, function(k) {
  x_k <- sum(results[seq_len(k)])
  a_k <- alpha0 + x_k
  b_k <- beta0 + k - x_k
  tibble(
    p = seq(0.001, 0.6, length.out = 400),
    density = dbeta(p, a_k, b_k),
    n_obs = k,
    x_obs = x_k,
    post_mean = a_k / (a_k + b_k),
    label = paste0("n = ", k, ", x = ", x_k)
  )
})

Bayes Estimators and Decision Theory

Point Estimation from the Posterior

The posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})\) is a full probability distribution. How do we extract a single point estimate?

. . .

Answer: it depends on your loss function. A Bayes estimator minimizes posterior expected loss:

\[\hat{\theta}_{\text{Bayes}} = \arg\min_{\delta} \, E[L(\theta, \delta) \mid \mathbf{x}] = \arg\min_{\delta} \int L(\theta, \delta) \, \pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \, d\theta\]

. . .

ImportantThree Key Results
Loss function \(L(\theta, \delta)\) Bayes estimator
Squared error \((\theta - \delta)^2\) Posterior mean \(E[\theta \mid \mathbf{x}]\)
Absolute error \(|\theta - \delta|\) Posterior median
0–1 loss \(\mathbf{1}(\theta \neq \delta)\) Posterior mode (MAP)

Derivation: Posterior Mean Minimizes Squared Error Loss

We want to find \(\delta^*\) that minimizes:

\[E[(\theta - \delta)^2 \mid \mathbf{x}] = \int (\theta - \delta)^2 \pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \, d\theta\]

. . .

Add and subtract the posterior mean \(\mu_{\text{post}} = E[\theta \mid \mathbf{x}]\):

\[E[(\theta - \delta)^2 \mid \mathbf{x}] = E[(\theta - \mu_{\text{post}} + \mu_{\text{post}} - \delta)^2 \mid \mathbf{x}]\]

\[= \underbrace{E[(\theta - \mu_{\text{post}})^2 \mid \mathbf{x}]}_{\text{Var}(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})} + (\mu_{\text{post}} - \delta)^2 + 2(\mu_{\text{post}} - \delta)\underbrace{E[\theta - \mu_{\text{post}} \mid \mathbf{x}]}_{=\, 0}\]

\[= \text{Var}(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) + (\mu_{\text{post}} - \delta)^2\]

. . .

This is minimized when \(\delta^* = \mu_{\text{post}} = E[\theta \mid \mathbf{x}]\). \(\qquad \square\)

Bayes Estimator: Beta–Binomial Case

For \(p \mid x \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + x, \, \beta + n - x)\):

\[\hat{p}_{\text{Bayes}} = E[p \mid x] = \frac{\alpha + x}{\alpha + \beta + n}\]

. . .

Let \(n_0 = \alpha + \beta\) (the “prior sample size”) and \(p_0 = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) (the prior mean). Then:

\[\hat{p}_{\text{Bayes}} = \frac{n_0}{n_0 + n} \cdot p_0 + \frac{n}{n_0 + n} \cdot \hat{p}_{\text{MLE}}\]

. . .

This is a convex combination (weighted average) of the prior mean and the MLE:

  • Weight on prior \(= n_0/(n_0 + n)\) — decreases as \(n\) grows
  • Weight on MLE \(= n/(n_0 + n)\) — increases as \(n\) grows

. . .

NoteConnection to Shrinkage Estimation

This “shrinkage toward the prior” is related to James–Stein estimation and ridge regression in linear models. Bayesian estimators systematically trade a little bias for reduced variance — often beneficial in finite samples.

Key Philosophical Points

Subjectivity vs. Objectivity

  • The prior is subjective — two analysts with different priors reach different posteriors
  • Frequentist methods are also not fully “objective” (choice of test statistic, significance level, etc.)
  • With large data, both approaches give similar answers

. . .

The Bayesian Framework Naturally Handles:

  • Sequential analyses: posterior updates naturally as data accumulate (no multiple-testing correction needed for planned sequential designs)
  • Prediction: posterior predictive distribution \(f(\tilde{x} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \int f(\tilde{x} \mid \theta) \pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) d\theta\)
  • Complex models: hierarchical models, latent variable models via MCMC

. . .

NoteIn Your Career

You will encounter Bayesian methods in: - Adaptive clinical trials (FDA Bayesian guidance documents) - Hierarchical / mixed-effects models (often estimated via MCMC) - Biomarker analysis, genomics, imaging (high-dimensional priors) - Machine learning (Bayesian optimization, Gaussian processes, probabilistic neural networks)

Frequentist Properties of Bayesian Estimators

ImportantBernstein–von Mises Theorem

Under regularity conditions, as \(n \to \infty\):

\[\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\left(\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}},\; [I_n(\hat\theta)]^{-1}\right)\]

That is, the posterior concentrates around the MLE and becomes approximately normal — regardless of the prior.

. . .

Consequences:

  • For large samples, Bayesian and frequentist estimates agree
  • The \(95\%\) credible interval is asymptotically identical to the \(95\%\) Wald CI
  • The prior only matters in small samples or with strongly informative priors

. . .

Admissibility: Under squared error loss, the Bayes estimator is admissible (cannot be uniformly improved). This connects to decision theory — Bayesian procedures often have excellent frequentist risk properties.

Summary

Key Takeaways: Bayesian Inference

ImportantThe Fundamental Workflow

\[\underbrace{\pi(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} \xrightarrow{\text{observe data}} \underbrace{\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto L(\theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)}_{\text{posterior}}\]

. . .

Core concepts:

  • Prior encodes pre-data beliefs; can be informative, weakly informative, or flat
  • Posterior is the complete inferential summary — it is the answer to “what do we know about \(\theta\)?”
  • Conjugate priors give closed-form posteriors: Beta–Binomial, Normal–Normal, Gamma–Poisson
  • Credible intervals: direct probability statements about \(\theta\) — the interpretation frequentist CIs can’t make
  • Bayes estimators: minimize posterior expected loss; posterior mean (sq. error), median (abs. error), mode (0–1 loss)
  • Consistency: by Bernstein–von Mises, posteriors concentrate around the MLE as \(n \to \infty\)

Looking Forward

The tools from this quarter — MLE theory, sufficiency, sampling distributions, hypothesis testing — all appear in the Bayesian framework too:

  • The likelihood \(L(\theta)\) is the same object we have been maximizing
  • Sufficient statistics reduce the posterior: \(\pi(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \pi(\theta \mid T(\mathbf{x}))\)
  • MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo) generalizes bootstrap simulation for complex posteriors
  • Bayesian adaptive designs, hierarchical models, and posterior predictive checks are standard tools in biostatistics

. . .

  • Stan/brms: modern probabilistic programming for Bayesian models in R
