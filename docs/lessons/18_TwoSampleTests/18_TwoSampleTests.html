Two-Sample Setup and Point Estimation

The Two-Sample Model

Setup: Two independent random samples from possibly different populations:

\[X_{11}, X_{12}, \ldots, X_{1n_1} \stackrel{iid}{\sim} F_1 \quad \text{with mean } \mu_1, \text{ variance } \sigma_1^2\]

\[X_{21}, X_{22}, \ldots, X_{2n_2} \stackrel{iid}{\sim} F_2 \quad \text{with mean } \mu_2, \text{ variance } \sigma_2^2\]

The two samples are independent of each other.

. . .

Parameter of interest: \(\Delta = \mu_1 - \mu_2\)

Point estimator: \(\hat{\Delta} = \bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2\)

. . .

Properties of \(\hat{\Delta}\):

  • \(E[\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2] = \mu_1 - \mu_2 \Rightarrow\) unbiased
  • \(\text{Var}(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) = \dfrac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\) (by independence)
  • \(\text{SE}(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}\)

Why Is \(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2\) Approximately Normal?

Case 1: Both populations are normal — exact result for any \(n_1, n_2\):

\[\bar{X}_1 \sim N\!\left(\mu_1, \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}\right), \quad \bar{X}_2 \sim N\!\left(\mu_2, \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\right)\]

A linear combination of independent normals is normal, so \(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2 \sim N\!\left(\mu_1-\mu_2,\; \sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2\right)\) exactly.

. . .

Case 2: Non-normal populations, large \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) — CLT applies separately to each sample mean:

\[\bar{X}_1 = \frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_1} X_{1j} \;\dot{\sim}\; N\!\left(\mu_1, \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}\right) \quad \text{(CLT, as } n_1 \to \infty\text{)}\]

\[\bar{X}_2 = \frac{1}{n_2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_2} X_{2j} \;\dot{\sim}\; N\!\left(\mu_2, \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\right) \quad \text{(CLT, as } n_2 \to \infty\text{)}\]

Because the two samples are independent, \(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2\) is a sum of two asymptotically independent normal quantities:

\[\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2 \;\dot{\sim}\; N\!\left(\mu_1 - \mu_2,\; \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\right)\]

. . .

Note

Both \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) must be large — if one is small, the CLT approximation may be poor for that group, undermining the overall approximation. A common rule of thumb is \(n_1 \geq 30\) and \(n_2 \geq 30\).

The Pivot: Standardizing the Difference

In either case (exact or approximate), after standardizing:

\[Z = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}} \;\sim\; N(0,1)\]

This is a pivotal quantity when \(\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) are known — its distribution does not depend on any unknown parameters.

. . .

We can invert it to get a CI, just as in the one-sample case:

\[P\!\left(-z_{\alpha/2} \leq \frac{(\bar{X}_1-\bar{X}_2)-\Delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1+\sigma_2^2/n_2}} \leq z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha\]

\[\Rightarrow \quad (\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}\]

Two-Sample z-Procedures (\(\sigma\) Known)

Two-Sample z-Test and CI (DBC 9.5)

Two-sample z-test of \(H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \Delta_0\):

\[Z = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \Delta_0}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}}\]

Reject \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha\):

Alternative Rejection Region
\(\mu_1 - \mu_2 > \Delta_0\) \(Z > z_\alpha\)
\(\mu_1 - \mu_2 < \Delta_0\) \(Z < -z_\alpha\)
\(\mu_1 - \mu_2 \neq \Delta_0\) \(|Z| > z_{\alpha/2}\)

Two-sample z-CI (95%, two-sided): \[(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}\]

Medical Example: Two-Sample z-Test

Study: A pilot study compares mean serum cortisol (\(\mu\)g/dL) between ICU patients with sepsis (\(n_1 = 30\), known \(\sigma_1 = 6\)) and non-septic ICU controls (\(n_2 = 35\), known \(\sigma_2 = 5\)). Observed: \(\bar{x}_1 = 28.4\), \(\bar{x}_2 = 22.1\). Test \(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\) vs. \(H_a: \mu_1 > \mu_2\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

xbar1 <- 28.4; xbar2 <- 22.1
sigma1 <- 6;   sigma2 <- 5
n1 <- 30;      n2 <- 35

se_diff <- sqrt(sigma1^2/n1 + sigma2^2/n2)
z_obs   <- (xbar1 - xbar2) / se_diff
p_value <- pnorm(z_obs, lower.tail = FALSE)  # one-sided

z_crit <- qnorm(0.975)
ci_lo  <- (xbar1 - xbar2) - z_crit * se_diff
ci_hi  <- (xbar1 - xbar2) + z_crit * se_diff

cat("SE:", round(se_diff, 3), "| Z:", round(z_obs, 3),
    "| One-sided p:", round(p_value, 5), "\n")
SE: 1.384 | Z: 4.553 | One-sided p: 0 
cat("95% CI for mu1 - mu2: (", round(ci_lo, 2), ",", round(ci_hi, 2), ")\n")
95% CI for mu1 - mu2: ( 3.59 , 9.01 )

Conclusion: \(Z = 4.55\), \(p < 0.001\). Strong evidence that septic ICU patients have higher mean cortisol.

Two-Sample t-Procedures (\(\sigma\) Unknown)

The Behrens-Fisher Problem

In practice \(\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) are rarely known. Replacing them with sample variances \(S_1^2\), \(S_2^2\) gives the statistic:

\[T = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \Delta_0}{\sqrt{S_1^2/n_1 + S_2^2/n_2}}\]

Problem: \(T\) does not follow an exact \(t\) distribution when \(\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2\) — the numerator and denominator are not independent in the way needed for an exact \(t\) pivot. This is the Behrens-Fisher problem.

. . .

Two solutions:

Approach Assumption Null distribution
Welch (Satterthwaite) \(\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2\) allowed \(T \approx t_{\hat\nu}\)
Pooled \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2\) required \(T_P \sim t_{n_1+n_2-2}\) (exact)

Welch’s t-Test — Recommended Default (DBC 9.5)

Welch’s t-statistic: \[T_W = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \Delta_0}{\sqrt{S_1^2/n_1 + S_2^2/n_2}}\]

Under \(H_0\), \(T_W \approx t_{\hat\nu}\) where the Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom are:

\[\hat\nu = \frac{(S_1^2/n_1 + S_2^2/n_2)^2}{\dfrac{(S_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1} + \dfrac{(S_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}\]

Note: \(\hat\nu\) is always between \(\min(n_1, n_2)-1\) and \(n_1+n_2-2\).

. . .

Welch 95% CI for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\): \[(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,\hat\nu}\,\sqrt{\frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2}}\]

ImportantWhy Welch?

The Welch test is robust to unequal variances and performs well even when \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\). It is the default in R’s t.test() and recommended by most statisticians as the standard two-sample test.

The Pooled t-Test (DBC 9.5)

When \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2\) (equal variances assumed), we estimate the common variance by pooling:

\[S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2 + (n_2-1)S_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

\(S_p^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) and uses all \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) degrees of freedom.

Pooled t-statistic: \[T_P = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \Delta_0}{S_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} \sim t_{n_1+n_2-2} \quad \text{(exact under normality + equal } \sigma^2\text{)}\]

Pooled 95% CI: \[(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n_1+n_2-2}\;S_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}\]

WarningWhen to Use Pooled t

Only use the pooled t when there is strong prior subject-matter reason to believe \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\). Do not use an F-test on the same data to justify pooling — this inflates Type I error.

Why the Direction of Bias Matters

When \(\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2\), \(S_p^2\) is a weighted average of two estimators targeting different quantities. The pooled SE can be either too big or too small:

\[S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2 + (n_2-1)S_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2} \approx \frac{(n_1-1)\sigma_1^2 + (n_2-1)\sigma_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}\]

The true SE of \(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2\) is \(\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}\). Whether the pooled SE over- or underestimates this depends on the \(n\)–\(\sigma\) relationship:

Configuration Pooled SE vs. true SE Pooled test
\(n_1 = n_2\) (balanced) Approximately correct regardless of \(\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2\) \(\approx\) 5%
Larger group has larger \(\sigma\) (Scenario 3 below) Pooled SE too large → \(T\) deflated Conservative (< 5%)
Larger group has smaller \(\sigma\) (Scenario 4 below) Pooled SE too small → \(T\) inflated Anti-conservative (> 5%)

. . .

Note

The Welch test is robust to all of these scenarios because it uses \(S_1^2/n_1 + S_2^2/n_2\) directly without pooling.

Welch vs. Pooled: Type I Error Simulation

Code 
set.seed(551)
sim_ttest <- function(n1, n2, sigma1, sigma2, B = 3000) {
  map_dfr(1:B, function(i) {
    x1 <- rnorm(n1, mean = 0, sd = sigma1)
    x2 <- rnorm(n2, mean = 0, sd = sigma2)
    tibble(
      p_welch  = t.test(x1, x2, var.equal = FALSE)$p.value,
      p_pooled = t.test(x1, x2, var.equal = TRUE)$p.value
    )
  })
}

# Compute true SE and pooled-approx SE for annotation
true_se   <- function(n1, n2, s1, s2) sqrt(s1^2/n1 + s2^2/n2)
pooled_se <- function(n1, n2, s1, s2) {
  sp <- sqrt(((n1-1)*s1^2 + (n2-1)*s2^2) / (n1+n2-2))
  sp * sqrt(1/n1 + 1/n2)
}

scenarios <- tibble(
  n1     = c(20, 20, 10, 30),
  n2     = c(20, 20, 30, 10),
  sigma1 = c( 2,  1,  1,  1),
  sigma2 = c( 2,  3,  3,  3),
  label  = c(
    "Equal n, equal variances\nn1=n2=20, s1=s2=2",
    "Equal n, unequal variances\nn1=n2=20, s1=1, s2=3",
    "Larger group has larger variance\nn1=10, n2=30, s1=1, s2=3",
    "Larger group has smaller variance\nn1=30, n2=10, s1=1, s2=3"
  )
)

results <- pmap_dfr(scenarios, function(n1, n2, sigma1, sigma2, label) {
  sim_ttest(n1, n2, sigma1, sigma2) |>
    summarise(Welch  = mean(p_welch  < 0.05),
              Pooled = mean(p_pooled < 0.05)) |>
    mutate(
      Scenario  = label,
      true_se   = true_se(n1, n2, sigma1, sigma2),
      pooled_se = pooled_se(n1, n2, sigma1, sigma2),
      se_bias   = case_when(
        abs(pooled_se - true_se) < 0.02 ~ "Pooled SE ~ True SE",
        pooled_se > true_se             ~ "Pooled SE too LARGE\n(conservative)",
        TRUE                            ~ "Pooled SE too SMALL\n(anti-conservative)"
      )
    )
})

# Annotation data: only label the pooled bar
pooled_annot <- results |>
  select(Scenario, se_bias) |>
  mutate(Test = "Pooled", x_pos = 1)

plot_data <- results |>
  pivot_longer(c(Welch, Pooled), names_to = "Test", values_to = "TypeI")

ggplot(plot_data, aes(x = Test, y = TypeI, fill = Test)) +
  geom_col(width = 0.5) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0.05, linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_text(
    data = filter(plot_data, Test == "Pooled"),
    aes(label = se_bias, y = TypeI + 0.008),
    color = "#c0392b", size = 3.5, lineheight = 0.9
  ) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("Welch" = "#006a4e", "Pooled" = "#e74c3c")) +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::percent, limits = c(0, 0.30),
                     expand = expansion(mult = c(0, 0.05))) +
  facet_wrap(~Scenario, nrow = 1) +
  labs(
    title    = "Type I Error Rate: Welch vs. Pooled t-test  (nominal alpha = 5%)",
    subtitle = paste0(
      "Pooled test can be conservative OR anti-conservative depending on which group has the larger variance.\n",
      "Welch test stays near 5% in all scenarios."
    ),
    x = NULL, y = "Type I Error Rate", fill = "Method"
  ) +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom",
        strip.text    = element_text(size = 10),
        axis.text.x   = element_blank(),
        axis.ticks.x  = element_blank())

Pooled t-Test: DBC Example

DBC 9.5: Two care groups measured for systolic blood pressure (mmHg). Prior clinical knowledge supports equal variability.

  • Standard care: \(n_1 = 12\), \(\bar{x}_1 = 140.2\), \(s_1 = 14.3\)
  • Intensive care: \(n_2 = 10\), \(\bar{x}_2 = 128.7\), \(s_2 = 13.8\)
n1 <- 12; xbar1 <- 140.2; s1 <- 14.3
n2 <- 10; xbar2 <- 128.7; s2 <- 13.8

sp2 <- ((n1-1)*s1^2 + (n2-1)*s2^2) / (n1 + n2 - 2)
sp  <- sqrt(sp2)

t_obs <- (xbar1 - xbar2) / (sp * sqrt(1/n1 + 1/n2))
df    <- n1 + n2 - 2
p_val <- 2 * pt(abs(t_obs), df = df, lower.tail = FALSE)
t_crit <- qt(0.975, df = df)
margin <- t_crit * sp * sqrt(1/n1 + 1/n2)

cat(sprintf("Sp = %.2f,  df = %d\n", sp, df))
Sp = 14.08,  df = 20
cat(sprintf("T_obs = %.3f,  p-value = %.4f\n", t_obs, p_val))
T_obs = 1.908,  p-value = 0.0709
cat(sprintf("95%% CI: (%.2f, %.2f)\n",
            xbar1 - xbar2 - margin, xbar1 - xbar2 + margin))
95% CI: (-1.07, 24.07)

Conclusion: \(t = 1.908\), \(df = 20\), \(p = 0.0709\). Reject \(H_0\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\); evidence of a difference in systolic BP between care groups.

When Do Welch and Pooled t-Distributions Differ?

Code 
# Scenario A: similar n and similar s (Welch ~ Pooled)
nA1 <- 45; nA2 <- 43; sA1 <- 18; sA2 <- 12
nuA_welch  <- (sA1^2/nA1 + sA2^2/nA2)^2 /
              ((sA1^2/nA1)^2/(nA1-1) + (sA2^2/nA2)^2/(nA2-1))
nuA_pooled <- nA1 + nA2 - 2

# Scenario B: very unequal n and s (Welch << Pooled)
nB1 <- 8;  nB2 <- 40; sB1 <- 15; sB2 <- 3
nuB_welch  <- (sB1^2/nB1 + sB2^2/nB2)^2 /
              ((sB1^2/nB1)^2/(nB1-1) + (sB2^2/nB2)^2/(nB2-1))
nuB_pooled <- nB1 + nB2 - 2

t_grid <- seq(-5, 5, length.out = 600)

make_df <- function(nu_w, nu_p, label) {
  bind_rows(
    tibble(t = t_grid,
           density = dt(t_grid, df = nu_w),
           Method  = sprintf("Welch (df=%.1f)", nu_w)),
    tibble(t = t_grid,
           density = dt(t_grid, df = nu_p),
           Method  = sprintf("Pooled (df=%d)", nu_p))
  ) |> mutate(Scenario = label)
}

df_both <- bind_rows(
  make_df(nuA_welch, nuA_pooled,

                  nA1, nA2, sA1, sA2)),
  make_df(nuB_welch, nuB_pooled,
          sprintf("Scenario B: small n1, very unequal variances\nn1=%d, n2=%d, s1=%.0f, s2=%.0f",
                  nB1, nB2, sB1, sB2))
)

# Show T_obs = 2 in each panel (representative value in rejection region)

Sample Size for Two-Sample t-Test

Summary: Which Two-Sample Test?

Condition Method Degrees of Freedom
\(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2\) known; large \(n\) or normal Two-sample \(z\) \(\infty\) (use \(z_{\alpha/2}\))
\(\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2\), unknown (default) Welch \(t\) \(\hat\nu\) (Satterthwaite)
\(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\), strong prior knowledge Pooled \(t\) \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\)
Non-normal, small \(n\) Bootstrap or Wilcoxon rank-sum —

. . .

ImportantRules of Thumb
  • Default: Use Welch. It performs well whether or not variances are equal.
  • Pooled: Only when there is strong external (prior) subject-matter justification for equal variances — not based on testing the same data.
  • Checking normality: Plot the data (histogram, Q-Q plot). For \(n_i \geq 30\), the CLT provides protection; for small \(n\), departures from normality matter more.

Two-Sample Inference for Proportions (DBC 9.6)

Setup: Two Independent Bernoulli Samples

Group 1: \(X_{11}, \ldots, X_{1n_1} \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p_1)\); count \(Y_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n_1, p_1)\)

Group 2: \(X_{21}, \ldots, X_{2n_2} \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p_2)\); count \(Y_2 \sim \text{Bin}(n_2, p_2)\)

Parameter of interest: \(\Delta = p_1 - p_2\). Estimator: \(\hat\Delta = \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = Y_1/n_1 - Y_2/n_2\)

. . .

Why CLT applies: Each \(\hat{p}_i = Y_i/n_i\) is a sample mean of iid \(\text{Bernoulli}(p_i)\) observations. By CLT:

\[\hat{p}_i = \frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i} X_{ij} \;\dot{\sim}\; N\!\left(p_i,\; \frac{p_i(1-p_i)}{n_i}\right) \quad \text{for large } n_i\]

Since the groups are independent, the difference of two approximately normal quantities is approximately normal:

\[\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \;\dot{\sim}\; N\!\left(p_1 - p_2,\; \frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}\right)\]

. . .

NoteLarge-Sample Condition

The normal approximation is adequate when \(n_i\hat{p}_i \geq 5\) and \(n_i(1-\hat{p}_i) \geq 5\) for both \(i = 1, 2\). For rare events or small \(n\), use Fisher’s exact test or a bootstrap CI.

CI and Test for \(p_1 - p_2\) (DBC 9.6)

Wald CI (plug in \(\hat{p}_1, \hat{p}_2\) for unknown \(p_1, p_2\)): \[(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}\]

Hypothesis test of \(H_0: p_1 = p_2\): Under \(H_0\) both groups share a common \(p\). Estimate it as \(\hat{p} = (Y_1 + Y_2)/(n_1 + n_2)\) — the pooled proportion.

Pooled z-statistic: \[Z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1} + \dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\]

. . .

ImportantPooled vs. Unpooled SE
  • Test: Use pooled \(\hat{p}\) — \(H_0\) says \(p_1 = p_2\), so exploit that constraint.
  • CI: Use unpooled \(\hat{p}_1, \hat{p}_2\) — the CI makes no assumption about \(p_1 = p_2\).

Medical Example: Adverse Event Rates

Clinical Trial: A drug trial compares 30-day SAE rates: treatment (\(n_1 = 150\), \(y_1 = 12\)) vs. control (\(n_2 = 148\), \(y_2 = 24\)). Test \(H_0: p_1 = p_2\) and construct a 95% CI.

Relative Risk and Odds Ratio

The difference \(p_1 - p_2\) (risk difference) is one effect measure. Medical research often reports:

\[\text{Relative Risk (RR)} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}, \qquad \text{Odds Ratio (OR)} = \frac{p_1/(1-p_1)}{p_2/(1-p_2)}\]

CIs for RR and OR are constructed on the log scale (approximately normal), then back-transformed.

RR <- p1_hat / p2_hat
OR <- (p1_hat/(1-p1_hat)) / (p2_hat/(1-p2_hat))

# Delta-method SE on log scale
se_logRR <- sqrt(1/y1 - 1/n1 + 1/y2 - 1/n2)
se_logOR <- sqrt(1/y1 + 1/(n1-y1) + 1/y2 + 1/(n2-y2))

cat(sprintf("RR = %.3f  (95%% CI: %.3f, %.3f)\n",
    RR, exp(log(RR) - 1.96*se_logRR), exp(log(RR) + 1.96*se_logRR)))
RR = 0.493  (95% CI: 0.256, 0.949)
cat(sprintf("OR = %.3f  (95%% CI: %.3f, %.3f)\n",
    OR, exp(log(OR) - 1.96*se_logOR), exp(log(OR) + 1.96*se_logOR)))
OR = 0.449  (95% CI: 0.216, 0.936)

. . .

Interpretation: The treatment arm had approximately half the SAE rate of control (RR ≈ 0.49). When \(p\) is small, RR \(\approx\) OR; they diverge as event rates increase.

Inferences About Two Variances (DBC 9.6)

The F-Distribution

When comparing variability between two groups, we need the F-distribution.

Key result: If \(U \sim \chi^2_m\) and \(V \sim \chi^2_n\) are independent, then: \[F = \frac{U/m}{V/n} \sim F_{m,n}\]

Connection to sample variances: Under normality, \((n_i-1)S_i^2/\sigma_i^2 \sim \chi^2_{n_i-1}\) independently, so:

\[F = \frac{S_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{S_2^2/\sigma_2^2} \sim F_{n_1-1,\;n_2-1}\]

Under \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\), the ratio simplifies to \(F = S_1^2/S_2^2\).

. . .

Properties of \(F_{m,n}\):

  • Support: \((0, \infty)\); right-skewed for small \(m, n\)
  • \(E[F] = n/(n-2)\) for \(n > 2\)
  • Reciprocal: if \(F \sim F_{m,n}\) then \(1/F \sim F_{n,m}\)
  • Critical values in R: qf(p, df1, df2)

F-Test for \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\) (DBC 9.6)

Test statistic: \(f = s_1^2/s_2^2\); under \(H_0\), \(F = S_1^2/S_2^2 \sim F_{n_1-1,\,n_2-1}\)

Alternative Rejection Region p-value
\(\sigma_1^2 > \sigma_2^2\) \(f > F_{\alpha,\,n_1-1,\,n_2-1}\) \(P(F \geq f)\)
\(\sigma_1^2 < \sigma_2^2\) \(f < F_{1-\alpha,\,n_1-1,\,n_2-1}\) \(P(F \leq f)\)
\(\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2\) \(f < F_{1-\alpha/2}\) or \(f > F_{\alpha/2}\) \(2\min(P(F \leq f),\,P(F \geq f))\)

CI for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\):

\[\left(\frac{s_1^2/s_2^2}{F_{\alpha/2,\,n_1-1,\,n_2-1}},\;\; \frac{s_1^2/s_2^2}{F_{1-\alpha/2,\,n_1-1,\,n_2-1}}\right)\]

Note: use the reciprocal property \(F_{1-\alpha/2,\,m,n} = 1/F_{\alpha/2,\,n,m}\).

WarningThe F-test is Extremely Sensitive to Non-Normality

The F-test for equality of variances assumes exact normality of both populations — far more sensitive than the t-test for means. For non-normal data:

  • Levene’s test (car::leveneTest()) — uses absolute deviations from group medians; robust to non-normality
  • Bartlett’s test (bartlett.test()) — equivalent power to F-test but also assumes normality

In medical research, the F-test for variances is most appropriate when normality is well-established (e.g., duplicate laboratory measurements). In most other settings, Levene’s test is preferred.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways: Means (DBC 9.5)

ImportantTwo-Sample Tests for Means

  1. Point estimator \(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2\) is unbiased; approximately normal by CLT (both \(n_i\) large) or exact normality of populations

  2. Welch t-test (default): \(T_W \approx t_{\hat\nu}\); Satterthwaite df accounts for unequal variances; valid whether or not \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\)

  3. Pooled t-test: exact under normality + \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\); requires strong prior justification — anti-conservative when variances differ and \(n\)’s are unequal

  4. CI-test duality: the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\Delta\) contains exactly the \(\Delta_0\) values for which the two-sided test fails to reject at level \(\alpha\)

Key Takeaways: Proportions and Variances (DBC 9.6)

ImportantTwo-Sample Tests for Proportions and Variances

  1. Proportions (CLT): \(\hat{p}_i\) is a sample mean of Bernoulli observations; CLT gives \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \dot\sim N(p_1-p_2, \text{SE}^2)\); requires \(n_i\hat{p}_i \geq 5\) and \(n_i(1-\hat{p}_i) \geq 5\)

  2. Pooled vs. unpooled SE: Use pooled \(\hat{p}\) for the test (exploits \(H_0: p_1 = p_2\)); use unpooled for the CI (no such assumption)

  3. F-test: \(S_1^2/S_2^2 \sim F_{n_1-1,\,n_2-1}\) under normality and \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\); the CI for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\) is asymmetric

  4. Sensitivity: The F-test for variances is extremely sensitive to non-normality — prefer Levene’s test in practice for most biomedical data

  5. Always report point estimates + CIs; choose the effect measure (RD, RR, OR) that is most interpretable in context
