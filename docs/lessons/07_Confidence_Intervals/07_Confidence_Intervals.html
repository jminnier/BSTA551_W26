Lesson 7: From Point Estimates to Intervals

Review: What We’ve Learned About Estimation

Point Estimation (Lessons 1-6):

  • Estimators: statistics that estimate parameters
  • Properties: bias, variance, MSE, efficiency
  • Construction: Method of Moments, Maximum Likelihood
  • Quality: MVUE, sufficient statistics, Cramér-Rao bound

The Limitation of Point Estimates:

A single number (\(\hat{\theta} = 0.75\)) tells us nothing about precision or uncertainty of the estimate.

Motivating Example: Clinical Trial

A new antihypertensive drug is tested in \(n = 36\) patients. The mean reduction in systolic blood pressure is \(\bar{x} = 12.3\) mmHg.

Point estimate: \(\hat{\mu} = 12.3\) mmHg

Questions we can’t answer with just the point estimate:

  • How close is 12.3 to the true mean reduction?
  • Is the true mean likely to be at least 10 mmHg?
  • How much could \(\bar{X}\) vary in repeated trials?

The Solution

A confidence interval provides a range of plausible values for the parameter, along with a measure of confidence.

The Fundamental Idea

From Point to Interval

Key insight: The interval captures our uncertainty about the true parameter value.

What Is a Confidence Interval?

Definition: Confidence Interval

A confidence interval (CI) for a parameter \(\theta\) is a random interval \((L, U)\) computed from sample data such that: \[P(L < \theta < U) = 1 - \alpha\] for a specified confidence level \(1 - \alpha\).

Important:

  • \(L\) and \(U\) are random (they depend on the sample)
  • \(\theta\) is fixed (but unknown)
  • The probability statement is about the random event that the lower and upper bound cover \(\theta\), not about any randomness in \(\theta\) itself.
  • The probability statement only makes sense when thinking about defining the estimators \(L\) and \(U\) and their sampling distributions, it does not make sense when talking about one sample and the observed values for \(L\) and \(U\)

Correct Interpretation of Confidence Intervals

Common Misconception

“There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) lies in the interval (7.4, 17.2).”

This is WRONG! After computing the interval, \(\mu\) either is or is not in the interval. There’s no probability involved.

Correct Interpretation

“If we repeated this procedure many times, 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true \(\mu\).”

Or more simply/loosely: “We are 95% confident that the true mean lies between 7.4 and 17.2.”

Common Mistakes to Avoid (Chihara Example 7.2)

Suppose a 95% CI for mean gas mileage is (29.5, 33.4) mpg. Evaluate:

  1. ❌ “We are 95% confident that gas mileage for cars in this company is between 29.5 and 33.4 mpg.”
    • Wrong: CI is for population mean, not individual values.
  1. ❌ “95% of all samples will give an average mileage between 29.5 and 33.4 mpg.”
    • Wrong: Each sample gives a different CI; 95% of intervals contain \(\mu\).
  1. ❌ “There is a 95% chance that the true mean is between 29.5 and 33.4 mpg.”
    • Wrong: \(\mu\) is fixed, not random. It’s either in the interval or not.

Visualizing Confidence Level: Simulation

# Simulate 100 confidence intervals
set.seed(551)
true_mu <- 10; sigma <- 4; n <- 25

ci_data <- tibble(sample_id = 1:100) |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(
    xbar = mean(rnorm(n, true_mu, sigma)),
    lower = xbar - 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
    upper = xbar + 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n),
    contains_mu = lower < true_mu & upper > true_mu
  )

# Count how many contain the true mean
sum(ci_data$contains_mu)
[1] 94

Pivotal Quantities

The Key to Constructing CIs

Definition: Pivotal Quantity

A pivotal quantity is a function \(h(X_1, \ldots, X_n; \theta)\) of the data and parameter such that:

  1. It depends on both the sample and \(\theta\)
  2. Its distribution does not depend on \(\theta\) (or any other unknown parameters)

Why pivotal quantities matter:

  • We can find critical values that don’t depend on unknowns
  • We can “solve for \(\theta\)” to get confidence limits

Example: The Standard Pivotal Quantity

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma\) known.

Consider: \[Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\]

Check the properties:

  1. \(Z\) depends on \(\bar{X}\) (data) and \(\mu\) (parameter) ✓
  2. \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\) — the distribution doesn’t depend on \(\mu\) ✓

\(Z\) is a pivotal quantity!

From Pivotal Quantity to CI

Since \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\), we know: \[P(-z_{\alpha/2} < Z < z_{\alpha/2}) = 1 - \alpha\]

Substitute \(Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\): \[P\left(-z_{\alpha/2} < \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} < z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1 - \alpha\]

Solve for \(\mu\): \[P\left(\bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu < \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1 - \alpha\]

This gives us the confidence interval formula!

What is \(L\) and \(U\) here?

The General Pivotal Quantity Method

Recipe for Constructing CIs

  1. Find a pivotal quantity \(h(X_1, \ldots, X_n; \theta)\)
  2. Find constants \(a\) and \(b\) such that \(P(a < h < b) = 1 - \alpha\)
  3. Solve the inequalities for \(\theta\) to get \((L, U)\)

Example (Devore 8.1): For normal data with known \(\sigma\):

  • Pivotal: \(Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)\)
  • Constants: \(a = -z_{\alpha/2}\), \(b = z_{\alpha/2}\)
  • Solve: \(\bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu < \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

CI for Mean: \(\sigma\) Known

The Z-Interval

Z Confidence Interval for Normal Mean (\(\sigma\) Known)

If \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma\) known, a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\mu\) is: \[\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\] where \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the critical value with \(P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \alpha/2\).

Common critical values:

Confidence Level \(\alpha\) \(\alpha/2\) \(z_{\alpha/2}\)
90% 0.10 0.05 1.645
95% 0.05 0.025 1.960
99% 0.01 0.005 2.576

Example: Tensile Strength (Devore Example 8.1)

Synthetic fibers are tested for tensile strength. From past data, \(\sigma = 55\) MPa is known. A sample of \(n = 31\) fibers has \(\bar{x} = 1064\) MPa.

95% Confidence Interval:

xbar <- 1064
sigma <- 55
n <- 31
alpha <- 0.05

z_crit <- qnorm(1 - alpha/2)
margin <- z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n)

c(xbar - margin, xbar + margin)
[1] 1044.639 1083.361

Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true mean tensile strength is between 1044.6 and 1083.4 MPa.

Medical Example: Blood Glucose Levels

A hospital monitors fasting blood glucose in diabetic patients. Historical data shows \(\sigma = 18\) mg/dL. A sample of \(n = 40\) patients has \(\bar{x} = 142\) mg/dL.

Construct a 99% CI for the population mean glucose level:

xbar <- 142
sigma <- 18
n <- 40
alpha <- 0.01

z_crit <- qnorm(1 - alpha/2)
cat("z critical value:", round(z_crit, 3), "\n")
z critical value: 2.576 
margin <- z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n)
cat("Margin of error:", round(margin, 2), "mg/dL\n")
Margin of error: 7.33 mg/dL
cat("99% CI: (", round(xbar - margin, 1), ",", round(xbar + margin, 1), ")")
99% CI: ( 134.7 , 149.3 )

Clinical interpretation: We can be 99% confident that the true mean fasting glucose for this patient population is between 134.7 and 149.3 mg/dL.

Anatomy of a Confidence Interval

\[\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

Three factors affect the margin of error:

  1. Confidence level (\(z_{\alpha/2}\)): Higher confidence → wider interval → less precise
  2. Population variability (\(\sigma\)): More variation → wider interval → less precise
  3. Sample size (\(n\)): Larger sample → narrower interval → more precise

Interactive: Effect of Confidence Level

# Same data, different confidence levels
xbar <- 1064; sigma <- 55; n <- 31

confidence_levels <- c(0.90, 0.95, 0.99)

tibble(conf_level = confidence_levels) |>
  mutate(
    z_crit = qnorm(1 - (1 - conf_level)/2),
    margin = z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n),
    lower = xbar - margin,
    upper = xbar + margin,
    width = upper - lower
  )
# A tibble: 3 × 6
  conf_level z_crit margin lower upper width
       <dbl>  <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1       0.9    1.64   16.2 1048. 1080.  32.5
2       0.95   1.96   19.4 1045. 1083.  38.7
3       0.99   2.58   25.4 1039. 1089.  50.9

Takeaway: Higher confidence requires wider intervals!

Visualizing the Trade-off

Sample Size Determination

Question: How large should \(n\) be to achieve a desired precision?

If we want the margin of error to be at most \(b\): \[z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \leq b\]

Solving for \(n\): \[n \geq \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma}{b}\right)^2\]

Sample Size Example: Clinical Trial Design

A pharmaceutical company wants to estimate mean drug clearance time. From pilot data, \(\sigma \approx 25\) minutes. They want a 95% CI with margin of error ≤ 5 minutes.

sigma <- 25
b <- 5  # desired margin of error
alpha <- 0.05
z_crit <- qnorm(1 - alpha/2)

n_required <- (z_crit * sigma / b)^2
cat("Minimum sample size:", ceiling(n_required))
Minimum sample size: 97

Note: Always round up to ensure the desired precision!

Sample Size: Effect of Requirements

# How does required n change with margin of error and confidence?
expand_grid(
  margin = c(3, 5, 10),
  conf_level = c(0.90, 0.95, 0.99)
) |>
  mutate(
    z = qnorm(1 - (1 - conf_level)/2),
    n_required = ceiling((z * 25 / margin)^2)
  ) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = margin, values_from = n_required, 
              names_prefix = "margin_") |>
  select(-z)
# A tibble: 3 × 4
  conf_level margin_3 margin_5 margin_10
       <dbl>    <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
1       0.9       188       68        17
2       0.95      267       97        25
3       0.99      461      166        42

Key insight: Halving the margin of error (from 10 to 5) quadruples the sample size!

One-Sided Confidence Bounds

Sometimes we only care about one direction:

  • Lower bound: We want to know the minimum plausible value
  • Upper bound: We want to know the maximum plausible value

One-Sided Bounds

Lower Confidence Bound: \(\bar{x} - z_{\alpha} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu\)

Upper Confidence Bound: \(\mu < \bar{x} + z_{\alpha} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Note: Use \(z_\alpha\) (not \(z_{\alpha/2}\)) for one-sided bounds at confidence level \(1 - \alpha\).

Why \(z_\alpha\) instead of \(z_{\alpha/2}\)?

For a two-sided CI, we split \(\alpha\) into two tails (\(\alpha/2\) each): \[P\left(-z_{\alpha/2} < \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} < z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1 - \alpha\]

For a one-sided lower bound, all \(\alpha\) goes in one tail: \[P\left(\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} < z_{\alpha}\right) = 1 - \alpha\]

Rearranging for \(\mu\): \[P\left(\bar{X} - z_{\alpha} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu\right) = 1 - \alpha\]

So the lower bound is: \(L = \bar{X} - z_{\alpha} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Key insight: One-sided bounds use \(z_\alpha\) because we put all the “error probability” \(\alpha\) on one side, not split between two sides.

Medical Example: Drug Half-Life

A pharmacologist measures half-life of a new drug. With \(n = 20\) subjects, \(\bar{x} = 4.2\) hours, and known \(\sigma = 0.8\) hours.

Find a 95% lower confidence bound:

xbar <- 4.2
sigma <- 0.8
n <- 20
alpha <- 0.05

z_crit <- qnorm(1 - alpha)  # Note: z_alpha, not z_alpha/2
lower_bound <- xbar - z_crit * sigma / sqrt(n)

cat("95% lower confidence bound:", round(lower_bound, 2), "hours\n")
95% lower confidence bound: 3.91 hours
cat("Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true half-life",
    "\nis at least", round(lower_bound, 2), "hours")
Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true half-life 
is at least 3.91 hours

Why Lower vs. Upper Bounds?

Use a LOWER bound when:

  • Testing if a treatment effect is “at least” some value
  • Establishing minimum efficacy
  • Setting safety margins

Use an UPPER bound when:

  • Estimating maximum risk or adverse event rate
  • Establishing worst-case scenarios
  • Quality control (maximum defect rate)

Example: Remission Time (Devore 8.7)

A surgeon wants a lower bound for mean remission time after cancer surgery. From \(n = 25\) patients, \(\bar{x} = 42\) months, \(\sigma = 12\) months (from historical data).

n <- 25

We are 95% confident that true mean remission time

is at least 38.1 months.

Pivotal Quantities for Other Distributions

Example: Exponential Distribution (Devore 8.5)

Time-to-breakdown of insulating fluid follows \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) with mean \(1/\lambda\) and variance \(1/\lambda^2\). Given \(n = 10\) observations with \(\sum x_i = 550.87\) min.

The pivotal quantity: \[h = 2\lambda \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim \chi^2_{2n}\]

Why is this pivotal?

  • Depends on data (\(\sum X_i\)) and parameter (\(\lambda\))
  • Distribution (\(\chi^2_{2n}\)) doesn’t depend on \(\lambda\)

Exponential CI Derivation

Let \(\chi^2_{.025, 20}\) and \(\chi^2_{.975, 20}\) be the critical values. Then:

\[P\left(\chi^2_{.975, 20} < 2\lambda \sum X_i < \chi^2_{.025, 20}\right) = 0.95\]

Solving for \(\lambda\): \[P\left(\frac{\chi^2_{.975, 20}}{2\sum X_i} < \lambda < \frac{\chi^2_{.025, 20}}{2\sum X_i}\right) = 0.95\]

sum_x <- 550.87
lower <- qchisq(0.025, df = 20) / (2 * sum_x)
upper <- qchisq(0.975, df = 20) / (2 * sum_x)
cat("95% CI for λ: (", round(lower, 4), ",", round(upper, 4), ")")
95% CI for λ: ( 0.0087 , 0.031 )

CI for Mean of Exponential

Since the mean of \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) is \(\mu = 1/\lambda\):

# CI for mean breakdown time
mean_lower <- 1/upper
mean_upper <- 1/lower

cat("95% CI for mean breakdown time:\n")
95% CI for mean breakdown time:
cat("(", round(mean_lower, 1), ",", round(mean_upper, 1), ") minutes")
( 32.2 , 114.9 ) minutes

Note: The interval is quite wide — reflecting variability in the data and small sample size.

Summary and Looking Ahead

Lesson 7 Summary

Key Concepts:

  1. Confidence Intervals provide a range of plausible values with quantified uncertainty

  2. Correct Interpretation: “95% of intervals constructed this way contain \(\theta\)”

  3. Pivotal Quantities allow CI construction without knowing unknown parameters

  4. Z-intervals (\(\sigma\) known): \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

  5. Sample Size: \(n \geq \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma}{b}\right)^2\)

  6. One-sided bounds use \(z_\alpha\) instead of \(z_{\alpha/2}\)

Key Formulas for Z-Intervals

Quantity Formula
Two-sided CI \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Lower bound \(\bar{x} - z_{\alpha}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Upper bound \(\bar{x} + z_{\alpha}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Sample size \(n = \left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma}{b}\right)^2\)
Width \(w = 2 z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
