Lesson 5: Sufficient Statistics

Review: Where We Left Off

Key concepts from Lessons 1-4:

  • Point estimation: \(\hat{\theta}\) estimates unknown parameter \(\theta\)

  • MVUE: Unbiased estimator with minimum variance
  • Fisher Information: \(I(\theta) = E[-\ell''(\theta)]\)
  • Cramér-Rao Lower Bound: \(\text{Var}(T) \geq 1/I_n(\theta)\)
  • MLE and MME: Systematic methods for constructing estimators

. . .

Today’s Goals:

  • Understand sufficient statistics: data reduction without information loss
  • Learn the Neyman Factorization Theorem
  • Connect sufficiency to finding MVUEs via Rao-Blackwell
  • Define completeness and its role in optimal estimation

Motivating Question: Data Reduction

Suppose we observe \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) from a distribution with unknown \(\theta\).

Question: Can we summarize the data with a simpler statistic \(T(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) without losing information about \(\theta\)?

. . .

The Key Insight

If a statistic captures all the information about \(\theta\) contained in the data, we call it sufficient for \(\theta\).

. . .

Why this matters:

  • Simplifies analysis and computation
  • Guides us to optimal estimators
  • Fundamental for understanding MVUEs

The Concept of Sufficiency

Intuition: What Does “Sufficient” Mean?

Imagine you’re a consulting statistician. A colleague ran an experiment and wants your help estimating \(\theta\).

Scenario 1: They give you all raw data: \(x_1 = 1, x_2 = 0, x_3 = 3\)

Scenario 2: They only tell you the total: \(T = x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 4\)

. . .

Question: Does Scenario 1 give you more information about \(\theta\) than Scenario 2?

. . .

If the answer is no—if knowing \(T\) tells you everything about \(\theta\)—then \(T\) is sufficient for \(\theta\).

Example: Poisson Defects (Devore 7.25)

Setup: The number of major defects on new vehicles follows \(\text{Poisson}(\mu)\). We observe \(n = 3\) vehicles with defects \(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 0, X_3 = 3\).

Key Question: Would knowing only \(T = \sum X_i = 4\) provide the same information about \(\mu\) as knowing all individual \(X_i\)’s?

. . .

Consider: Given that \(T = 4\), what’s the conditional distribution of \((X_1, X_2, X_3)\)?

For the specific triple \((2, 1, 1)\):

\[P(X_1 = 2, X_2 = 1, X_3 = 1 | T = 4) = \frac{P(X_1 = 2, X_2 = 1, X_3 = 1)}{P(T = 4)}\]

Example: Poisson (continued)

\[P(X_1 = 2, X_2 = 1, X_3 = 1 | T = 4) = \frac{\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^2}{2!} \cdot \frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^1}{1!} \cdot \frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^1}{1!}}{\frac{e^{-3\mu}(3\mu)^4}{4!}}\]

. . .

\[= \frac{e^{-3\mu}\mu^4 / 2}{e^{-3\mu}(3\mu)^4/24} = \frac{24}{2 \cdot 81} = \frac{4}{27}\]

. . .

Critical Observation

The \(\mu\) terms cancel out! The conditional probability doesn’t depend on \(\mu\).

This means: once we know \(T = 4\), the specific \((X_1, X_2, X_3)\) values provide no additional information about \(\mu\).

Formal Definition of Sufficiency

Definition (Devore 7.3)

A statistic \(T = t(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) is sufficient for \(\theta\) if the conditional distribution of \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) given \(T = t\) does not depend on \(\theta\), for every possible value \(t\).

. . .

In other words: Once you know the value of \(T\), the distribution of the data no longer involves \(\theta\)—so no additional information about \(\theta\) can be extracted.

. . .

Intuition

\(T\) is sufficient if it “captures” all the information in the data relevant to \(\theta\). It’s sufficient to know \(T\); the raw data adds nothing more.

Why is Sufficiency Important?

Practical Implications:

  1. Data Reduction: Sufficient statistics compress data without information loss

  2. Estimator Selection: Any good estimator should depend on the data only through sufficient statistics

  3. Path to MVUE: Combined with other properties, leads to minimum variance estimators

. . .

Historical Note: The concept was developed by R.A. Fisher in the 1920s as part of the foundations of modern statistical inference.

The Neyman Factorization Theorem

Finding Sufficient Statistics: The Challenge

The definition of sufficiency requires computing conditional distributions—which can be quite difficult!

. . .

We need a shortcut…

. . .

Fortunately, the Neyman Factorization Theorem provides an elegant criterion based on the likelihood function.

The Neyman Factorization Theorem

Theorem (Neyman-Fisher Factorization)

Let \(f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta)\) denote the joint pmf or pdf of \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\). Then \(T = t(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\) if and only if there exist functions \(g\) and \(h\) such that:

\[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta) = g(t(x_1, \ldots, x_n); \theta) \cdot h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\]

where:

  • \(g\) depends on the data only through \(t(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) and involves \(\theta\)
  • \(h\) depends on the data but not on \(\theta\)

Understanding the Factorization

\[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta) = \underbrace{g(t(x_1, \ldots, x_n); \theta)}_{\text{Involves } \theta \text{ and data through } T} \cdot \underbrace{h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}_{\text{Depends on data, not } \theta}\]

. . .

Key insight: All information about \(\theta\) is in the \(g\) factor, which depends on \(x_i\)’s only through \(T\).

. . .

Strategy for Finding Sufficient Statistics
  1. Write down the joint pmf/pdf
  2. Identify the part that involves \(\theta\)
  3. Factor it so \(\theta\) appears only with a function of the data
  4. That function is your sufficient statistic!

Example 1: Bernoulli Distribution

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(p)\)

Joint pmf: \[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; p) = \prod_{i=1}^n p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i} = p^{\sum x_i}(1-p)^{n - \sum x_i}\]

. . .

Factorization: \[= \underbrace{p^t(1-p)^{n-t}}_{g(t; p)} \cdot \underbrace{1}_{h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}\]

where \(t = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\).

. . .

Result

\(T = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) (equivalently, \(\bar{X}\)) is sufficient for \(p\).

Example 2: Poisson Distribution

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\mu)\)

Joint pmf: \[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \mu) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{x_i}}{x_i!} = \frac{e^{-n\mu}\mu^{\sum x_i}}{\prod_{i=1}^n x_i!}\]

. . .

Factorization: \[= \underbrace{e^{-n\mu}\mu^t}_{g(t; \mu)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^n x_i!}}_{h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}\]

where \(t = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\).

. . .

Result

\(T = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) is sufficient for \(\mu\).

Example 3: Exponential Distribution

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\) with pdf \(f(x; \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\)

Joint pdf: \[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i} = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}\]

. . .

Factorization: \[= \underbrace{\lambda^n e^{-\lambda t}}_{g(t; \lambda)} \cdot \underbrace{1}_{h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}\]

where \(t = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\).

. . .

Result

\(T = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) (equivalently, \(\bar{X}\)) is sufficient for \(\lambda\).

Example 4: Uniform Distribution (Devore 7.27)

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Uniform}[0, \theta]\)

Joint pdf: \[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\theta} \cdot I(0 \leq x_i \leq \theta) = \frac{1}{\theta^n} \cdot I(\max(x_i) \leq \theta) \cdot I(\min(x_i) \geq 0)\]

. . .

Factorization: Using indicator notation: \[= \underbrace{\frac{1}{\theta^n} I(\max(x_i) \leq \theta)}_{g(t; \theta)} \cdot \underbrace{I(\min(x_i) \geq 0)}_{h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}\]

where \(t = \max(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\).

. . .

Result

\(T = \max(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) is sufficient for \(\theta\). Note: \(\bar{X}\) is NOT sufficient here!

Uniform Example: Visualization

Only the maximum observation matters for estimating \(\theta\)!

Why Isn’t \(\bar{X}\) Sufficient for Uniform?

Consider two datasets with the same mean but different maxima:

# Two samples with same mean but different maxima
data_A <- c(2, 4, 6, 8)  # mean = 5, max = 8
data_B <- c(1, 4, 5, 10) # mean = 5, max = 10

cat("Dataset A: mean =", mean(data_A), ", max =", max(data_A), "\n")
Dataset A: mean = 5 , max = 8 
cat("Dataset B: mean =", mean(data_B), ", max =", max(data_B), "\n")
Dataset B: mean = 5 , max = 10

. . .

These have the same \(\bar{X} = 5\), but:

  • Dataset A tells us \(\theta \geq 8\)
  • Dataset B tells us \(\theta \geq 10\)

. . .

Knowing only \(\bar{X}\) loses information about \(\theta\)!

The maximum captures information that the mean cannot.

Your Turn: Finding Sufficient Statistics

Exercise: Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim \text{Geometric}(p)\) with pmf \(f(x; p) = (1-p)^{x-1}p\) for \(x = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\)

Find a sufficient statistic for \(p\).

. . .

Solution:

\[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; p) = \prod_{i=1}^n (1-p)^{x_i - 1}p = p^n (1-p)^{\sum x_i - n}\]

\[= \underbrace{p^n (1-p)^{t - n}}_{g(t; p)} \cdot \underbrace{1}_{h}\]

where \(t = \sum x_i\).

. . .

Result: \(T = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) is sufficient for \(p\).

Jointly Sufficient Statistics

When One Statistic Isn’t Enough

When there are multiple parameters, we typically need multiple sufficient statistics.

. . .

Definition: Jointly Sufficient Statistics

Statistics \(T_1, \ldots, T_k\) are jointly sufficient for parameters \(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_m\) if the conditional distribution of \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) given \(T_1 = t_1, \ldots, T_k = t_k\) does not depend on any of the \(\theta_j\)’s.

. . .

Factorization Theorem (Extended): \[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \theta_1, \ldots, \theta_m) = g(t_1, \ldots, t_k; \theta_1, \ldots, \theta_m) \cdot h(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\]

Example: Normal Distribution (Both Parameters Unknown)

Setup: \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with both \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) unknown.

Joint pdf: \[f(x_1, \ldots, x_n; \mu, \sigma^2) = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\right)^n \exp\left(-\frac{\sum(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

. . .

Expanding \(\sum(x_i - \mu)^2 = \sum x_i^2 - 2\mu \sum x_i + n\mu^2\):

\[= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sigma^n} \exp\left(-\frac{\sum x_i^2 - 2\mu \sum x_i + n\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

Normal Distribution (continued)

\[f = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma^n} \exp\left(-\frac{\sum x_i^2 - 2\mu \sum x_i + n\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}_{g(t_1, t_2; \mu, \sigma^2)} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^{n/2}}_{h}\]

. . .

Result

\(T_1 = \sum X_i\) (or \(\bar{X}\)) and \(T_2 = \sum X_i^2\) are jointly sufficient for \((\mu, \sigma^2)\).

Equivalently, \((\bar{X}, S^2)\) are jointly sufficient.

. . .

Intuition: For normal data, the sample mean and sample variance capture all information about \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).

Minimal Sufficiency

The Problem: Non-Uniqueness of Sufficient Statistics

Sufficient statistics are not unique!

. . .

Trivial example: The entire dataset \((X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) is always sufficient.

Less trivial: If \(T\) is sufficient, so is any one-to-one function of \(T\) (e.g., if \(\sum X_i\) is sufficient, so is \(\bar{X}\)).

. . .

Question: What is the “best” or “most efficient” sufficient statistic?

. . .

Idea

We want the sufficient statistic that achieves the greatest data reduction while still capturing all information about \(\theta\).

Definition: Minimal Sufficiency

Definition (Devore 7.3)

A sufficient statistic \(T\) is minimal sufficient if it is a function of every other sufficient statistic.

Equivalently: \(T\) is minimal sufficient if, for any other sufficient statistic \(T'\), we can write \(T = f(T')\) for some function \(f\).

. . .

Intuition: A minimal sufficient statistic represents the maximum possible data reduction without losing information about \(\theta\).

. . .

Example: For \(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with both unknown, \((\bar{X}, S^2)\) are jointly minimal sufficient—you cannot reduce the data any further without losing information.

Finding Minimal Sufficient Statistics

Practical Criterion (Devore Exercise 7.74)

\(T = t(X_1, \ldots, X_n)\) is minimal sufficient if for any two sample points \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) and \(\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)\):

\[\frac{f(\mathbf{x}; \theta)}{f(\mathbf{y}; \theta)} \text{ doesn't depend on } \theta \iff t(\mathbf{x}) = t(\mathbf{y})\]

. . .

In words: The likelihood ratio is constant in \(\theta\) if and only if the two samples have the same value of \(T\).

Example: Minimal Sufficiency for Exponential

For \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\):

\[\frac{f(\mathbf{x}; \lambda)}{f(\mathbf{y}; \lambda)} = \frac{\lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}}{\lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum y_i}} = e^{-\lambda(\sum x_i - \sum y_i)}\]

. . .

This ratio doesn’t depend on \(\lambda\) \(\iff\) \(\sum x_i = \sum y_i\).

. . .

Therefore: \(T = \sum X_i\) is minimal sufficient for \(\lambda\).

A Cautionary Example: Cauchy Distribution

The Cauchy distribution with location parameter \(\theta\) has pdf: \[f(x; \theta) = \frac{1}{\pi[1 + (x - \theta)^2]}\]

. . .

For a random sample from Cauchy:

  • The ordered values \((X_{(1)}, \ldots, X_{(n)})\) are jointly sufficient
  • Remarkably, the ordered values are also minimal sufficient!

. . .

Surprising Result

For Cauchy, there is no reduction from the ordered sample to a simpler statistic. The full ordered data is needed to capture all information about \(\theta\).

This shows that the number of minimal sufficient statistics doesn’t always equal the number of parameters.

The Rao-Blackwell Theorem

Why Sufficiency Matters for Estimation

We’ve established that sufficient statistics capture all information about \(\theta\).

Key Question: How do we use this to find better estimators?

. . .

The Rao-Blackwell Theorem provides the answer: it shows how to improve any estimator by conditioning on sufficient statistics.

The Rao-Blackwell Theorem

Theorem (Rao-Blackwell)

Suppose:

  • \(T\) is sufficient for \(\theta\)
  • \(U\) is any unbiased estimator for \(h(\theta)\)

Then the estimator \(U^* = E(U | T)\) satisfies:

  1. \(U^*\) is unbiased for \(h(\theta)\)
  2. \(\text{Var}(U^*) \leq \text{Var}(U)\)

with strict inequality unless \(U\) is already a function of \(T\).

. . .

In words: Conditioning an unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic never increases variance and often decreases it.

Proof Sketch: Why Rao-Blackwell Works

Step 1: \(U^*\) is an estimator (doesn’t depend on \(\theta\))

Since \(T\) is sufficient, the conditional distribution of \(U | T\) doesn’t involve \(\theta\). Therefore \(E(U | T)\) doesn’t involve \(\theta\). ✓

. . .

Step 2: \(U^*\) is unbiased

By the Law of Total Expectation: \[E(U^*) = E[E(U | T)] = E(U) = h(\theta) \checkmark\]

. . .

Step 3: \(U^*\) has smaller variance

By the Law of Total Variance: \[\text{Var}(U) = \text{Var}(E[U|T]) + E[\text{Var}(U|T)] = \text{Var}(U^*) + E[\text{Var}(U|T)]\]

Since \(E[\text{Var}(U|T)] \geq 0\), we have \(\text{Var}(U) \geq \text{Var}(U^*)\). ✓

Example: Poisson Zero-Defect Probability (Devore 7.30)

Problem: Estimate \(e^{-\mu} = P(\text{no defects})\) for \(X_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu)\).

Start with a crude unbiased estimator: \[U = I(X_1 = 0) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } X_1 = 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } X_1 > 0 \end{cases}\]

. . .

Check: \(E(U) = P(X_1 = 0) = e^{-\mu}\) ✓ (Unbiased!)

. . .

Apply Rao-Blackwell: The sufficient statistic is \(T = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\).

We need: \(U^* = E(U | T) = P(X_1 = 0 | T = t)\)

Poisson Example (continued)

\[P(X_1 = 0 | T = t) = \frac{P(X_1 = 0 \cap T = t)}{P(T = t)} = \frac{P(X_1 = 0) \cdot P(\sum_{i=2}^n X_i = t)}{P(T = t)}\]

. . .

Using the fact that \(\sum_{i=2}^n X_i \sim \text{Poisson}((n-1)\mu)\):

\[= \frac{e^{-\mu} \cdot \frac{e^{-(n-1)\mu}[(n-1)\mu]^t}{t!}}{\frac{e^{-n\mu}(n\mu)^t}{t!}} = \frac{(n-1)^t}{n^t} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^t\]

. . .

Improved Estimator

\[U^* = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^T\] is unbiased for \(e^{-\mu}\) with variance \(\leq\) the variance of the crude indicator!

Simulation: Comparing Estimators

Code 
set.seed(42)
mu_true <- 1.5
n <- 10
n_sims <- 5000

# Simulate Poisson samples and compute estimators
poisson_sim <- tibble(sim = 1:n_sims) |> 
  mutate(
    data = map(sim, \(s) rpois(n, mu_true)),
    T_sum = map_dbl(data, sum),
    crude_U = map_dbl(data, \(d) as.numeric(d[1] == 0)),  # I(X_1 = 0)
    improved_U = (1 - 1/n)^T_sum,  # Rao-Blackwell improved
    mle = exp(-T_sum/n)  # MLE of e^(-μ)
  )

# True value
true_prob <- exp(-mu_true)

# Compare estimators
comparison <- poisson_sim |> 
  summarize(
    `E(Crude U)` = mean(crude_U),
    `E(Improved U*)` = mean(improved_U),
    `E(MLE)` = mean(mle),
    `Var(Crude U)` = var(crude_U),
    `Var(Improved U*)` = var(improved_U),
    `Var(MLE)` = var(mle)
  )

cat("True P(X = 0) = e^(-μ) =", round(true_prob, 4), "\n\n")
True P(X = 0) = e^(-μ) = 0.2231

Key Implication: Best Estimators Use Sufficient Statistics

Corollary of Rao-Blackwell

Any estimator that is not based purely on sufficient statistics is necessarily inferior to some other estimator.

. . .

Example: For Uniform\([0, \theta]\), \(\max(X_i)\) is sufficient. Any estimator using \(\bar{X}\) or \(S\) alone is suboptimal:

  • \(\hat{\theta}_1 = 2\bar{X}\) has variance \(\theta^2/(3n)\)
  • \(\hat{\theta}_2 = \frac{n+1}{n}\max(X_i)\) has variance \(\theta^2/[n(n+2)]\)

Since \(\bar{X}\) isn’t a function of \(\max(X_i)\), estimator \(\hat{\theta}_1\) loses information!

MLEs and Sufficient Statistics

Theorem (MLEs Use Sufficient Statistics)

If \(T_1, \ldots, T_k\) are jointly sufficient for \(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_m\), then the MLEs \(\hat{\theta}_1, \ldots, \hat{\theta}_m\) can be expressed as functions of \(T_1, \ldots, T_k\) alone.

. . .

Why? The likelihood factors as \(L(\theta) = g(T; \theta) \cdot h(\mathbf{x})\). Maximizing \(L\) is equivalent to maximizing \(g\), which depends on the data only through \(T\).

. . .

Practical implication: MLEs automatically “use” sufficient statistics—this is one reason they have such good properties!

Completeness

The Final Piece: Completeness

We’ve seen that:

  • Sufficient statistics capture all information about \(\theta\)
  • Rao-Blackwell lets us improve estimators by conditioning on sufficient statistics

. . .

Question: Given a sufficient statistic \(T\), is there a unique best unbiased estimator based on \(T\)?

. . .

The answer involves completeness—a property that ensures uniqueness.

Definition of Completeness

Definition: Complete Statistic

A statistic \(T\) is complete for \(\theta\) if for any function \(g\):

\[E_\theta[g(T)] = 0 \text{ for all } \theta \implies P_\theta(g(T) = 0) = 1 \text{ for all } \theta\]

In other words, the only function of \(T\) with expected value 0 for all \(\theta\) is the zero function (almost surely).

. . .

Intuition: Completeness means there are “no redundancies” in \(T\)—you can’t construct a non-trivial unbiased estimator of zero using \(T\).

Why Completeness Matters

Lehmann-Scheffé Theorem

If \(T\) is a complete sufficient statistic and \(U = h(T)\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\), then \(U\) is the unique MVUE of \(\tau(\theta)\).

. . .

Proof Sketch:

Suppose \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are both unbiased for \(\tau(\theta)\) and are functions of \(T\).

Then \(E[U_1 - U_2] = 0\) for all \(\theta\).

By completeness, \(U_1 - U_2 = 0\) almost surely.

Therefore \(U_1 = U_2\)—uniqueness! ✓

Completeness in Exponential Families

Theorem

For distributions in the exponential family (which includes Normal, Poisson, Exponential, Binomial, Gamma), the natural sufficient statistics are typically complete.

. . .

Examples of complete sufficient statistics:

Distribution Complete Sufficient Statistic
Bernoulli\((p)\) \(\sum X_i\)
Poisson\((\mu)\) \(\sum X_i\)
Exponential\((\lambda)\) \(\sum X_i\)
Normal\((\mu, \sigma^2)\) \((\bar{X}, S^2)\) jointly

Example: Normal Mean (Known Variance)

For \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2\) known:

  • \(\bar{X}\) is sufficient for \(\mu\)
  • \(\bar{X}\) is complete for \(\mu\)
  • \(\bar{X}\) is unbiased for \(\mu\)

. . .

By Lehmann-Scheffé: \(\bar{X}\) is the unique MVUE of \(\mu\).

. . .

The Power of Completeness + Sufficiency

Once we find a complete sufficient statistic, any unbiased function of it is automatically the MVUE!

The Road to MVUE: Summary

Medical Research Applications

Application 1: Disease Incidence Rate

Scenario: Epidemiologists count disease cases in \(n\) geographic regions.

Let \(X_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu)\) = cases in region \(i\).

# Disease cases in 8 regions
disease_cases <- c(3, 5, 2, 7, 4, 6, 3, 5)
n <- length(disease_cases)

# Sufficient statistic
T_total <- sum(disease_cases)

# MVUE of μ
mu_hat <- mean(disease_cases)

# MVUE of P(no cases) = e^(-μ)
prob_zero_hat <- (1 - 1/n)^T_total

cat("Sufficient statistic T = Σxᵢ =", T_total, "\n")
Sufficient statistic T = Σxᵢ = 35 
cat("MVUE of μ:", mu_hat, "cases per region\n")
MVUE of μ: 4.375 cases per region
cat("Improved estimate of P(no cases):", round(prob_zero_hat, 4))
Improved estimate of P(no cases): 0.0093

Application 2: Drug Response Proportion

Scenario: In a clinical trial, we observe patient responses to treatment.

Let \(X_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\) where \(p\) = response rate.

# 50 patients: 1 = responded, 0 = no response
set.seed(111)
responses <- rbinom(50, 1, prob = 0.7)

# Sufficient statistic
T_sum <- sum(responses)

# MVUE of p (sample proportion)
p_hat <- mean(responses)

# 95% CI for p (using normal approximation)
se <- sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / length(responses))
ci <- p_hat + c(-1.96, 1.96) * se

cat("Sufficient statistic T = Σxᵢ =", T_sum, "responders\n")
Sufficient statistic T = Σxᵢ = 40 responders
cat("MVUE of p:", p_hat, "\n")
MVUE of p: 0.8 
cat("95% CI: (", round(ci[1], 3), ",", round(ci[2], 3), ")")
95% CI: ( 0.689 , 0.911 )

Scenario: Patient survival times follow Exponential\((\lambda)\).

# Survival times (months) for 20 patients
survival_times <- c(14.2, 8.5, 23.1, 11.7, 19.8, 6.3, 15.4, 12.9, 
                    25.6, 9.1, 18.3, 7.8, 21.4, 13.2, 16.7, 10.5,
                    22.8, 8.9, 17.6, 11.3)

# MLE/MVUE of λ

Summary and Looking Ahead

Lesson 5 Summary: Key Concepts

1. Sufficiency

  • A statistic \(T\) is sufficient if it captures all information about \(\theta\)
  • Formal criterion: conditional distribution of data given \(T\) doesn’t depend on \(\theta\)

. . .

2. Neyman Factorization Theorem

  • \(f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) = g(T; \theta) \cdot h(\mathbf{x})\)
  • Practical tool for identifying sufficient statistics

. . .

3. Rao-Blackwell Theorem

  • Conditioning on sufficient statistics improves estimators
  • Any estimator not based on sufficient statistics is suboptimal

. . .

4. Completeness

  • Combined with sufficiency, guarantees unique MVUE (Lehmann-Scheffé)

Sufficient Statistics: Quick Reference

Bernoulli\((p)\) \(p\) \(\sum X_i\) Yes
Poisson\((\mu)\) \(\mu\) \(\sum X_i\) Yes

Key Theorems Flowchart

To find MVUE:

  1. Find sufficient statistic \(T\) (Factorization Theorem)
  2. Check completeness (exponential family → typically complete)
  3. Find unbiased function of \(T\)
  4. Result: MVUE (by Lehmann-Scheffé)

. . .

Alternative:

  1. Compute Fisher Information \(I(\theta)\)
  2. Find CRLB = \(1/[nI(\theta)]\)
  3. Find estimator achieving CRLB → MVUE
